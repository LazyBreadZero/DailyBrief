The Protein Brewery's €30M Series B — Novo Holdings Bets the Ozempic Economy Needs Better Protein

Sources: AgFunderNews 9/10  ·  Silicon Canals 8/10  ·  Green Queen 8/10

The Protein Brewery, based in Breda in the southern Netherlands, has closed a €30 million Series B led by Novo Holdings — the investment arm of the Novo Nordisk Foundation, which funds the company that makes Ozempic. The round also includes Unovis Asset Management, and crucially, Invest-NL and the Brabant Development Agency: the same Dutch government-backed catalytic investor that anchors the Netherlands' position as the global centre of gravity for B2B fermentation infrastructure. This is not a coincidence. It is a thesis about what the GLP-1 economy requires next.

As established in Editions 2 and 3, the B2B Netherlands fermentation model — licensing infrastructure rather than building consumer brands — is the fundable architecture in the current alt-protein capital environment. Planetary SA (CHF 22m Series A, Invest-NL) and The Protein Brewery (€30m Series B, Invest-NL) now represent two consecutive, distinct data points confirming that thesis. The Netherlands cluster is no longer a hypothesis.

The Protein Brewery's product is Fermotein: a fungi-based protein powder produced from Rhizomucor pusillus under non-sterile fermentation conditions. The non-sterile process is the key differentiator. Most biomass fermentation competitors require sterile environments — capital-intensive reactors with rigorous contamination control. Rhizomucor pusillus tolerates open conditions, reducing both the capital cost per kilogram and the complexity of scale-up. The result is approximately 50% complete protein and 35% dietary fibre per weight, produced in a demo plant in Mijkenbroek at 600 metric tons per year — expandable to 2,500 MT at the same site. The Series B funds US and Singapore market entry.

But the most significant signal in this round is the lead investor. Novo Holdings manages the endowment of the Novo Nordisk Foundation. Novo Nordisk — the pharma giant behind Ozempic and Wegovy — has approximately 40 million people globally on GLP-1 agonists for weight management. The clinical picture on GLP-1 therapy is well-established: patients lose weight effectively but lose disproportionate lean muscle mass alongside fat, particularly without adequate dietary protein. High-quality, accessible protein supplementation is not a nice-to-have for GLP-1 users. It is a clinical requirement. Novo Holdings is not making a food trend bet. It is backing the nutritional complement to its foundation's anchor pharmaceutical asset.

For investors following the preventive health nutrition mandate — which is Calm/Storm's expanding thesis — this is the structural signal to anchor. The GLP-1 wave is not a competitor to food science investment. It is the demand driver that brings pharmaceutical-grade rigour to the nutrition market, and capital with it. Fermotein, priced at institutional ingredient scale, sits at the intersection of sports nutrition, clinical nutrition, and functional food — categories that Calm/Storm's mandate now explicitly includes.

Edition continuity note
The cultivated meat winter (Believer Meats, Meatable — $458m combined capital lost, covered Edition 3) deepens as capital flows to what survives: B2B fermentation infrastructure with industrial-grade demand. Three consecutive editions have now confirmed the same structural argument. The argument is now settled. Future coverage will track named commercial milestones — not re-establish the thesis.

"When the foundation funding the world's leading weight-loss drug backs your protein powder, it is not a food story. It is a metabolic health infrastructure story."

Seoul Food & Hotel Opens June 9 — Korea's Export Machine Comes to Market

Seoul Food & Hotel 2026 opens in three days, June 9–12 at KINTEX in Goyang, Gyeonggi Province. Now in its 44th edition, the exhibition will host 1,650 companies from 45 countries, and has adopted the theme "AI & Robotics: The Era of Food Tech Convergence" — a deliberate signal that South Korea is not merely exporting food culture. It is exporting food science methodology. The United States is this year's guest country of honour, a geopolitical positioning statement in the context of trade and food standard negotiations.

K-Food Plus exports reached $13.62 billion in 2025, with a $16 billion target for 2026. Europe is growing fastest: instant noodle exports to Europe exceeded $100 million for the first half of 2025, an all-time high, up 65.3% year-on-year. Nongshim Europe, established in Amsterdam in early 2025, is targeting the €1.8 billion European instant noodle market directly. The K-Food Fair Frankfurt — a parallel aT Center event connecting Korean exporters to DACH buyers — continues to expand. Korea's food export strategy has moved from cultural wave to industrial infrastructure, and its European distribution is now institutional, not viral.

Art Basel's VIP First Choice Preview opens in ten days, on June 16, with the main fair running June 18–21 at Messe Basel. The countdown matters structurally. This year's Basel Exclusive initiative means 193 of 232 main-sector galleries have formally committed to withholding major works from all pre-fair previews, online viewing rooms, and email communications until that Tuesday morning opening. Fermotein vs. BioBlocks — The Two Fermentation Architectures That Will Own the Protein Transition

Edition 4  ·  6 June 2026  ·  Approx. 1,000 words

Two Bets on the Same Market

Planetary SA and The Protein Brewery are frequently described as comparable companies. Both are Netherlands-anchored. Both received Invest-NL funding. Both are B2B. Both are working in biomass fermentation. Both rejected the cultivated meat path and the consumer brand path. The surface-level analysis — "two similar companies doing adjacent things" — is wrong in a way that matters for investment decisions. The architectures are fundamentally different, and the difference determines who is positioned to follow Novo Holdings' capital logic into scale.

BioBlocks: The Infrastructure Licensing Model

Planetary SA's BioBlocks system is a modular fermentation platform designed to convert agricultural side streams — specifically sugar industry residues — into mycoprotein. Planetary does not sell the protein. It licenses the fermentation technology to agro-industrial companies that already own the side streams. A sugar refinery, a starch manufacturer, a potato processor: these companies generate carbohydrate-rich waste streams with established logistics infrastructure. BioBlocks enables them to convert that waste into protein ingredients without building fermentation capability from scratch.

The business model is the distinguishing feature. Planetary takes a licensing fee and a royalty on production volume. It does not own bioreactors. It does not manage supply chain. It does not build brand. The capital requirement is concentrated in technology development and in enabling the first industrial partnerships — but the scale-up is funded by the licensee's balance sheet, not Planetary's. At ALDI Suisse price parity with conventional chicken, the BioBlocks model has demonstrated one non-trivial proof point: that mycoprotein produced via licensing can compete at retail on economics, not just nutrition.

The diligence questions for BioBlocks that a generalist cannot answer: What is the organism? (Trichoderma reesei, a workhorse industrial cellulase producer — not novel, not novel-food-pathway-constrained in most markets.) What are the downstream processing steps, and who owns the equipment? (Planetary licences the process; the licensee provides the downstream infrastructure.) How does the royalty stack compare to internal fermentation investment at scale? (This is the make-vs.-buy analysis that agro-industrial CFOs will run — and Planetary's answer needs to be more capital-efficient than building in-house.)

Fermotein: The Ingredient Brand Model

The Protein Brewery's model is different. It produces Fermotein — the branded ingredient — from its own facility. Rhizomucor pusillus is its organism of choice, and the non-sterile fermentation conditions it tolerates are the core IP. Where most biomass fermentation requires a sterile environment (contamination risk requires expensive clean-room reactors with rigorous monitoring), Rhizomucor pusillus thrives under conditions that other organisms would find hostile. This dramatically reduces capital expenditure per kilogram and simplifies scale-up: you are adding fermentation capacity, not rebuilding clean-room infrastructure.

The business model is ingredient supply. The Protein Brewery sells Fermotein to food manufacturers who use it in formulation — better-for-you bakery, snacking, clinical nutrition products. It owns its production facility in Mijkenbroek. It controls quality. It builds brand equity with ingredient buyers. The path to scale is conventional food ingredient supply chain development: qualify with tier-one food companies, establish specification sheets, build inventory depth, win long-term supply agreements.

The diligence questions: What is Rhizomucor pusillus's novel food status in target markets? (In the EU, this matters — though Rhizomucor is present in traditional European foods, including certain aged cheeses, which may simplify the regulatory argument.) What is the amino acid completeness of Fermotein at the 50% protein claim? (The 35% fibre co-production is an asset for certain applications but a formulation challenge in others.) How does the non-sterile process manage consistency? (Batch-to-batch amino acid and fibre profiles in non-sterile conditions require careful quality monitoring — this is where a food scientist's diligence goes.)

Why Novo Holdings Chose Fermotein

The Protein Brewery's model — ingredient producer with a branded product — is better suited to the clinical nutrition demand signal that Novo Holdings is capitalising on. GLP-1 users need standardised, high-quality protein at consistent specification. A licensing model (BioBlocks) creates specification variation across licensees. A controlled production facility (Fermotein) enables the pharmaceutical-grade consistency that clinical nutrition applications require. Novo Holdings is not just making a food bet. It is positioning Fermotein as a clinical ingredient that can be co-prescribed or co-recommended alongside GLP-1 therapy — a market that does not yet have a dominant player.

This does not make BioBlocks inferior. The two models serve different markets: BioBlocks scales by changing the economics of agro-industrial protein production globally; Fermotein scales by building specification trust in premium applications. In 2026's capital environment — where B2B traction and credible demand are the qualifying criteria — both have passed the test. But for Calm/Storm's nutrition-plus-health mandate, Fermotein's clinical nutrition pathway is the closer fit.

"The question for a food scientist doing diligence is not 'which organism?' but 'which regulatory pathway, at what capital intensity, for which buyer's specification sheet?' BioBlocks and Fermotein give completely different answers to each."
