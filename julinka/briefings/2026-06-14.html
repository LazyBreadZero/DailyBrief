A New Buyer Walks Onto the Fermentation Map: What the Pentagon's Alt-Protein Push Means for Calm/Storm's Diligence

Sunday, 14 June 2026 · ~1,000 words · DARPA's biomanufacturing programme and the US Army's $9M Biosphere contract for gas-fermentation protein

Every entity this brief has added to its fermentation-capital map so far has been a supplier: a company making an ingredient — casein, mycoprotein, heme, a fermentation-derived dye — for a food or beverage brand to put in front of a consumer. The map has tracked architectures (Editions 5, 8), geographies (Edition 8), regulatory pathways (Editions 9, 11) and, as of Edition 11, distressed infrastructure. What it has not yet had to account for is a structurally different kind of customer: one that does not care about shelf appeal, brand story or even taste in the conventional sense, and whose procurement decisions are not bound by the consumer-market timelines this brief has spent twelve editions calibrating. This week, that customer showed up. The US Department of Defense — through DARPA and the Army — is now an active funder of fermentation-protein technology, with at least one named contract: $9M to a company called Biosphere, to produce "gas protein" for military rations.

Why "Gas Protein" Is Not a Gimmick

Gas fermentation — growing microbes on gases such as hydrogen, CO2 or methane rather than on sugars or agricultural feedstocks — has been a fringe architecture in this brief's map until now, mentioned only in passing relative to the sugar-fed and waste-stream-fed systems (Standing Ovation, MATR Foods, Pacifico Biolabs) that dominate the commercial pipeline. The Pentagon's interest reorders that hierarchy, for a reason specific to its use case: a gas-fed bioreactor does not need a supply chain of agricultural inputs. Reporting on the DARPA programme describes the long-term ambition explicitly — equipment small enough to fit on "the back of a truck," eventually smaller still, that could let troops "make their own food" in the field using a biomanufacturing process. For a consumer-facing investor, feedstock cost and availability are a unit-economics question. For the military, feedstock independence is the entire point: a field unit that can convert ambient gases into edible protein does not need a resupply convoy for that component of its rations. The "third architecture" framing this brief introduced for Pacifico Biolabs in Edition 5 — repurposed brewery-tank infrastructure as an arbitrage on existing capital stock — now has a sibling: gas fermentation as an arbitrage on logistics, for a buyer that prices logistics risk in a currency (lives, mission failure) that has no equivalent on a consumer P&L.

This matters for the map in a very literal way. Biosphere is not a company this brief has previously tracked, and the $9M figure is small next to the raises this brief usually records — Standing Ovation's €25M Series B (Edition 10), MATR Foods' €40M (Edition 8), Astanor's nine-figure fund (Edition 11). But venture-scale and defense-procurement-scale are different instruments measuring different things. A $9M Army contract is not a valuation signal; it is a demand signal from a buyer with a near-unlimited budget ceiling, a multi-decade time horizon, and — crucially — no exposure to the EU novel-food versus US GRAS asymmetry that today's lead story just made concrete. Military rations are not subject to FDA GRAS notification or EFSA Novel Food authorisation in the way a retail product is; defense procurement runs its own qualification process. A fermentation company that can satisfy a defense buyer's specifications may reach revenue years before an equivalent consumer-facing product clears either regulatory pathway this brief has spent three editions mapping.

The Pattern, Not Just the Anecdote

Treat Biosphere as a single data point, but treat the pattern it represents as the thing worth tracking. Deep-tech categories that struggle to find consumer-market product-market fit on the originally pitched timeline have, historically, often found an earlier and more forgiving first customer in defense, aerospace or another mission-driven procurement budget — GPS, the internet, lithium-ion batteries and a long list of materials science breakthroughs all had a government-procurement phase before a consumer one. Cultivated meat and precision-fermented proteins have spent the last eighteen months of this brief's coverage absorbing exactly the kind of bad news — Believer Meats' collapse (Edition 3), the UPSIDE/Believer fire-sale (Editions 11), the EU's regulatory gap (Edition 11, today's lead) — that historically precedes a category looking for an alternative first buyer. DARPA's interest did not appear in response to that bad news, but it is arriving at the moment the category most needs a demand source that does not run on the same clock as EFSA or a retail buyer's category review.

For Calm/Storm, the immediate implication is not "go find a defense contract" — that is not the fund's mandate, and grafting a defense-procurement thesis onto a preventive-health and nutrition fund would be exactly the kind of unforced positioning drift Julinka should be wary of in her own portfolio mapping (see Angle 3 below, on Calm/Storm's and Astanor's adjacent-category moves). The implication is diagnostic: when assessing any fermentation company's go-to-market plan, "who is the realistic first paying customer, on what timeline, under what regulatory regime" is now a three-branch question, not a two-branch one. Consumer retail (slow, EU-blocked per today's lead), foodservice/B2B ingredient supply (the model most of this brief's portfolio companies have pursued), and now — for companies whose process characteristics happen to overlap with defense specifications — government procurement, which moves on a third clock entirely, indifferent to both EFSA and the FDA. A company whose technology happens to sit at that intersection (gas fermentation, modular/field-deployable bioreactors, shelf-stable protein formats) has an exit from the EU regulatory bottleneck that today's lead story implies the rest of the category does not.

The honest caveat: this is one $9M contract and a research-agency interest statement, not a trend with multiple data points yet. The test for whether this becomes a real third branch of the map, rather than a curiosity, is whether a second defense-adjacent fermentation contract appears within the next few editions — and whether any company already on this brief's map (Pacifico Biolabs' repurposed-infrastructure thesis is the closest structural fit) turns up as a counterparty. Until then, the value of this story is the question it adds to the diligence list, not a conclusion it supports.

Formo's FDA "No Questions" Letter Lands — the US Clock From Edition 9 Just Stopped. Brussels' Hasn't Started.

FoodNavigator-USA 9/10   Flavorist 7/10   FDA GRAS Notice Inventory (GRN 1312) 10/10   Green Queen 8/10

Formo's recombinant αS1-casein has received its FDA "no questions" letter — GRN 1312 has cleared its final review stage. What Edition 11 described as "expected this year" has happened, roughly four weeks before Formo's planned commercial debut at IFT FIRST in Chicago (13–16 July). The FDA has confirmed it has no further safety queries about the GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) status Formo self-determined in December 2025 and notified the agency of in the new year. This is the formal end-point of the GRAS notification process — not a new development in the sense of a fresh announcement, but the resolution of the exact open thread Editions 6, 9 and 11 have tracked across two months.

Step back: "GRAS" is not one status but a sequence of at least four distinct stages, and the gap between them is where most of the diligence value sits. Stage one is self-determination — a company's own scientific panel concludes an ingredient is safe, with no regulatory filing at all. Stage two is notification — the company tells the FDA it has self-determined GRAS and submits its dossier (this is where Formo sat as of Edition 9, "in active scientific review"). Stage three is the review period itself, during which the FDA can ask questions, request more data, or object. Stage four — where Formo now sits — is the "no questions" letter: the FDA has reviewed the dossier and has nothing further to ask. None of these four stages is a formal "approval" in the way EFSA's Novel Food authorisation is, but stage four is the closest the US system gets to a green light, and it is the stage that unlocks confident commercial planning — exactly the kind of planning behind a 13–16 July trade-show debut. A portfolio company describing itself as "GRAS" without specifying which of these four stages it occupies is, functionally, withholding the most decision-relevant fact in the sentence.

Set against the EU side of Edition 11's "two regulatory clocks," the asymmetry is no longer a forecast — it is a completed comparison. On the US side: self-determination (December 2025) → notification → active review → no-questions letter (this week), a roughly six-month sequence with a named endpoint and a named product launch sitting on the other side of it. On the EU side: as of today, EFSA has issued zero Novel Food opinions for any precision-fermented protein, the Biotech Act's regulatory sandbox explicitly excludes novel foods (Edition 11), and no formal application from Formo, Standing Ovation or any comparable company has a published timeline at all. One side of the ledger now has a date stamped on it. The other side does not yet have a process that produces dates.

For Calm/Storm, Formo's sequence is now a template, not a hypothetical — and a useful one for benchmarking. Any European precision-fermentation company in the pipeline can be asked: where, specifically, in this four-stage sequence does your US filing sit, and what is your dated estimate for reaching stage four? Formo went from notification to a no-questions letter inside roughly six months. A portfolio or pipeline company that has been "in GRAS review" for longer than that without a stage-four letter is either earlier in a harder review (worth understanding why) or has not been precise about its own status — both findings worth having before the next portfolio review, not after.

CONTINUITY NOTE

As introduced in Edition 9 (11 June 2026), "two regulatory clocks" described Formo and Standing Ovation racing on parallel US and EU tracks. Reinforcement: one of those two clocks has now rung — Formo's US clock reached its terminal stage this week. The EU clock, by contrast, has not yet been observed to move at all in this brief's coverage. When the next EU regulatory headline in this beat arrives, the question is not "is this progress" but "is this the first sign the EU clock has actually started" — a much higher bar.

"A 'no questions' letter isn't a finish line so much as a starting gun — except in Brussels, where, as of this week, the gun still hasn't been manufactured."

A Second, Separate Set of Nature-Credit Pilots Is Already Running — France, Estonia and Peru, via "Green Assist"

Alongside the IUCN-led pilots in Croatia, the Netherlands and Poland (Edition 10), a second and distinct EU-backed pilot track — run through the Commission's "Green Assist" advisory initiative — is already live: wetland restoration in France, sustainable forest management in Estonia, and an exploratory partnership project in Peru. The Nature Credits Roadmap's expert group is due to deliver certification methodologies "by mid-2026" — i.e., now. Two parallel pilot tracks testing overlapping questions (governance, monitoring, financing models) is more activity than Edition 10 implied, but also a fragmentation risk worth flagging rather than treating as double the momentum. Sources: EU Commission Green Forum 10/10. Follow-up: whether the IUCN and Green Assist pilot tracks converge on shared methodology before the mid-2026 expert-group deadline, or run as parallel, potentially conflicting frameworks.

JULINKA'S ANGLE
Three concrete moves — anchored to this edition's news
1.

Formo's GRAS sequence is now a dated template — benchmark every fermentation pipeline company against it before Art Basel week swallows the calendar. Formo went from FDA notification to a "no questions" letter in roughly six months, with a named commercial debut (IFT FIRST, 13–16 July, ~4 weeks out) on the other side. For any pipeline company describing itself as "GRAS," ask precisely which of the four stages (self-determination / notification / active review / no-questions letter) it occupies, and get a dated estimate for the next stage. A company that has sat in "active review" longer than Formo did, without explanation, is either facing a harder review or has been imprecise about its status — either is worth knowing this week.

2.

Tuesday now has three claimants on one morning — First Choice, Zero 10's preview, Unlimited's open run. Pick the allocation today, not Tuesday. Unlimited (Katrib's "ecological conditions of the present," Edition 11) is accessible across the whole fair and doesn't need to be Tuesday at all — which frees the gated Tuesday morning for a straight choice between First Choice (exclusivity, Basel Exclusive reveals) and Zero 10's preview (digital/computational art, the closest art-world echo of this edition's Deep Read on field-deployable biomanufacturing). Decide which of those two is worth the Tuesday slot, and put Unlimited on a different day — that's three access points resolved with one decision instead of an open-ended one.

3.

Before your first week at Calm/Storm: write a half-page comparing SquareMind (Calm/Storm's first devices bet) to AgZen (Astanor's first non-fermentation bet) as twin instances of "adjacent-category drift." Two funds whose core theses this brief has mapped in detail — Calm/Storm's preventive-health focus, Astanor's agri-food-ocean focus — have each just made their first investment outside that core category, within weeks of each other. That's either a pattern (specialist funds widening as their categories mature) or a coincidence — but it's exactly the kind of cross-fund observation a food-scientist-turned-investor is positioned to make and nobody else at either fund is positioned to notice. A half-page memo on this, ready before your first week starts, is a genuinely original opening contribution — not a chore, a calling card.

SIGNAL

SIGNAL

NOISE

"Two new sets of nature-credit pilots means twice the momentum" — IUCN's three-country pilots (Edition 10) and Green Assist's France/Estonia/Peru pilots are separate tracks under the same Roadmap.
