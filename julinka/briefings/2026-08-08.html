Tate & Lyle shareholders back Ingredion’s £2.7 billion takeover with 98.6% of votes cast

At the court-convened general meeting on 28 July, Tate & Lyle shareholders voted 98.64% in favour of Ingredion’s all-cash offer of 595 pence per share — a 59% premium to the May 13 closing price. The vote effectively secures the deal’s shareholder leg. Completion remains subject to antitrust clearances across the UK, EU, United States, and several other jurisdictions, plus UK High Court sanction; the companies continue to expect the transaction to close in the second half of 2027. FoodNavigator 9/10 Globe Newswire 9/10

The combined group would unite Ingredion’s starch-derived specialty ingredients with Tate & Lyle’s sweeteners and texturising systems, targeting US$130 million in annual cost synergies by 2030. Ingredion’s CEO Jim Zallie said antitrust filings are “on track.” The EU and US legs are the substantive risks: the combined entity would hold dominant positions in several specialty-starch and texturiser categories where both companies compete and sell to the same food manufacturers. MLex 9/10

For food-tech investors, the deal creates a new structural fact: the combined Ingredion-T&L entity will be the single largest specialty-ingredients supplier in the categories most food-technology startups use to formulate products at scale — from texturisers in plant-based meat to modified starches in functional nutrition. Whether the consolidation narrows or simplifies supply access for early-stage companies in those categories depends on what the antitrust processes require to be divested. In prior large ingredient-company mergers, the remedy has typically been divesting overlapping product lines to a strategic buyer, which creates its own secondary market for ingredient technology.

The vote also advances the deal’s logic that has run through the archive since May: Ingredion has repeatedly “paid ahead of certainty” — the Benicaros novel-food dossier acquisition before Commission authorisation landed, the T&L offer before any antitrust review was complete. The 98.6% shareholder approval confirms that Ingredion’s board and investors believe the premium is worth the regulatory uncertainty ahead. Antitrust is now the only remaining variable that can stop it.

SCiFi Foods auction clears August 6–8; Believer Meats NC facility sale pending August 20

An online auction for the assets of San Leandro-based cultivated meat startup SCiFi Foods ran August 6–8, with equipment and intellectual property up for sale following the company’s wind-down. The Believer Meats NC production facility sale hearing is scheduled for August 20 (or August 17 auction if qualifying bids above UPSIDE Foods’ $50 million stalking-horse bid were received by the August 5 deadline); the Israeli IP auction, run separately by trustee Yoel Freilich (Gissin & Co) for Future Meat Technologies Ltd’s cell lines, media formulations, and process know-how, closed August 5 after attracting “vast interest from cultivated meat companies, investment funds, and other entities.” Results on both the FML IP and the NC facility sale remain to be confirmed at time of publication. AgFunderNews 9/10 AgFunderNews 9/10

Two separate liquidations resolving in the same week frame the current state of the cultivated meat sector more plainly than any funding report can. SCiFi Foods and Believer Meats (formerly Future Meat Technologies) together raised over $700 million. What is being auctioned is infrastructure and IP — the technology itself attracted bidders even after both businesses failed. That is not a verdict on the science; it is a verdict on the business models that tried to commercialise it. The buyers of the IP and equipment will be the ones who attempt the next model.

Watching: Believer Meats NC facility sale hearing August 20; FML IP buyer identity; whether any European precision fermentation company or corporate strategic acquires the Believer/SCiFi process know-how.
Julinka’s Angle

The Ingredion-T&L deal is now one regulatory hearing from closing. Before the EU antitrust filing produces its remedies list, map the Calm/Storm food-tech pipeline against one question for each company: does the combined Ingredion-T&L entity appear as a customer, a competitor, or a potential acquirer in a five-year exit scenario? A specialty-ingredients consolidation of this scale changes the buyer landscape for ingredient-dependent companies and narrows the number of strategic acquirers in the texturiser and specialty-starch space. If any Calm/Storm portfolio company’s commercial plan has Tate & Lyle or Ingredion as a named strategic partner or route-to-market, the deal’s antitrust outcome is a material variable in that company’s next funding narrative.

Separately: whoever buys the Believer or SCiFi cultivated meat IP is worth tracking. The technology retained value even after the businesses failed. If a European precision fermentation company or corporate strategic — not UPSIDE, which is a direct US cultivated meat competitor — acquires the process know-how, that is a signal about where consolidation capital sees residual option value in the sector.
