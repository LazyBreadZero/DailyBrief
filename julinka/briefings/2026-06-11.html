Two Regulatory Clocks: How Formo's FDA Filing and Brussels' Biotech Act II Just Redrew the Fermentation Map's Finish Line

Thursday, 11 June 2026 · ~1,050 words · Formo FDA GRAS active review, Berlin/Brussels

Edition 6 of this brief introduced the Formo–Standing Ovation casein race as a horse race: two well-funded European precision-fermentation companies, both chasing the same prize — the first commercially available animal-free casein in the United States — with the same rough finish line, IFT FIRST Chicago on 13–16 July. Edition 8 noted that both were still pre-commercial, and left the race open. This week it stopped being open. On 2 June, Formo notified the FDA that its recombinant casein has entered active GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) review — the only precision-fermented casein currently under live scientific review by the US regulator. Formo expects a "no questions" letter before the year is out. Standing Ovation, which raised $34.2M with backing from Danone, Bel and Bpifrance, is still targeting a GRAS "no questions" letter by end-2026 via its Ajinomoto partnership — but has not yet filed for active review.

That gap — "in active review" versus "targeting a future submission" — is the first material separation this brief has recorded between the two companies since the race was named. It did not happen by accident, and the reason it happened reveals something about how this category actually works that the horse-race framing obscured.

Compete on Product, Merge on Science

Formo's GRAS filing is not a solo effort. The casein under FDA review was co-developed with Those Vegan Cowboys, the Belgian precision-fermentation company that appeared in Edition 8's list of four companies sharing in the precision-fermented-dairy funding rebound. Formo and Those Vegan Cowboys are not merging as businesses — their consumer cheese products remain separate, developed independently, and will compete on supermarket shelves. What they pooled is the upstream science: a combined team of more than 60 scientists working jointly on strain engineering, bioprocessing and large-scale production of bioidentical casein, with the explicit goal of reaching cost parity with bovine casein faster than either company could alone.

This is a structure this brief has not seen before in the fermentation map built across Editions 5–8: pre-competitive collaboration on the hardest, most expensive, most regulatorily-scrutinised part of the stack — strain development and process scale-up — followed by independent, competitive go-to-market. Standing Ovation, by contrast, has pursued its GRAS pathway largely on its own (with Ajinomoto as a manufacturing partner, not a co-development partner on the regulatory dossier itself). The Formo/Those Vegan Cowboys structure effectively doubled the scientific resources thrown at the single hardest gating step — the GRAS dossier — without doubling the cost to either company. That is very plausibly why Formo got there first.

A Second Clock, Starting in Brussels

While the FDA clock advances in Formo's favour, a second regulatory clock just started ticking on the other side of the Atlantic — and it covers every company in this brief's fermentation map, not just the casein racers. On 10 June — yesterday — the European Commission's call for evidence on "Biotech Act II" closed. Biotech Act I covered health biotechnology; Act II is the Commission's first attempt at a cross-sectoral framework for industrial biotechnology and biomanufacturing, explicitly scoped to include fermentation and synthetic biology, with a legislative proposal targeted for Q4 2026. The Commission has already flagged its likely intervention areas: streamlining permitting and authorisation for bio-based products, harmonising interpretation across member states and EU agencies (the single biggest practical headache for any company trying to sell a fermentation-derived ingredient in more than one EU country today), and expanding regulatory sandboxes.

The submissions to that call for evidence — due yesterday — included a coordinated push from advocacy groups including WePlanet, explicitly asking the Commission to name microbial proteins, single-cell proteins and precision-fermentation ingredients in the scope of Act II, rather than leaving the category to be read in by inference from "industrial biotechnology" generally. The distinction matters enormously to every company in this brief's map. Planetary's BioBlocks licensing platform, MATR's solid-state fermentation plant, Standing Ovation's and Formo's casein — all of these are, today, navigating a patchwork of national interpretations of what "fermentation-derived food ingredient" means under existing EU food law. A Biotech Act II that names the category explicitly is the EU equivalent of an FDA GRAS "no questions" letter: not approval for any single product, but a regulatory precedent that every subsequent company in the category can point to.

Why the Map Needs a Third Axis

Edition 8 left Julinka with a two-axis matrix: feedstock-source geography on one axis, process architecture (licensing, ingredient-brand, infrastructure-arbitrage, waste-stream, solid-state) on the other. That matrix answers "what does this company make, and how." It does not answer the question that, as of this week, is doing more to determine who wins each regional market first than either axis: where does this company sit on the regulatory clock, in each jurisdiction it is targeting?

Formo and Standing Ovation occupy the same cell on Edition 8's matrix — both are casein-via-precision-fermentation companies with European manufacturing bases targeting US commercial launch. On the two-axis matrix, they are indistinguishable. On a third axis — US regulatory-pathway stage — they are now meaningfully different: one is in active FDA review with a "no questions" letter plausible this year, the other is still assembling its dossier. That difference is, right now, the single most decision-relevant fact about either company for anyone doing diligence on the casein category — more relevant than feedstock or process architecture, because it is the difference between "sellable in the US in 2026" and "sellable in the US in 2027 at the earliest."

The same logic applies in reverse for the EU. Every company in the Editions 5–8 map is, as of yesterday, in the same position on the Biotech Act II axis: post-call-for-evidence, pre-proposal, with the scope of "fermentation-derived ingredient" still undefined in EU law. That is a moment of maximum regulatory ambiguity — and also a moment when lobbying input (like WePlanet's submission) can still shape the eventual scope. A company, or a fund, that engages with that process now is buying influence over a definition that will determine market access for an entire category in 2027 and beyond. A company that waits for the Q4 2026 proposal is reading the definition, not writing it.

What This Changes at Calm/Storm

Two concrete implications. First, the architecture × geography matrix gets a third axis — regulatory-pathway stage, tracked separately for the US (FDA GRAS: not filed / self-affirmed / active review / "no questions" letter received) and the EU (pre- or post-Biotech Act II definition). For any two companies that look identical on the first two axes — as Formo and Standing Ovation currently do — this third axis is where the actual investment-timing decision lives. Second, the Formo/Those Vegan Cowboys structure — pre-competitive R&D pooling on the regulatory-gating step, competitive go-to-market after — is itself a thing to look for, and ask for, in Calm/Storm's own pipeline. Any portfolio or prospective company facing a GRAS-equivalent gating step in its category should be asked one direct question this week: is there a competitor you could merge science with, on just this step, without merging anything else? Formo's answer to that question is the reason it is currently winning a race this brief has been tracking for three editions.

Planetary Raises Again — $28M More Brings Total Funding to $40M, in a Structure That Looks Like MATR's

Planetary — introduced in Edition 2 with a CHF 22M Series A and the BioBlocks licensing model — has raised again: CHF 16M (~$20M) in equity plus CHF 6M (~$7.5M) in credit, taking total funding to roughly $40M. The round was led by returning investors Radikal Capital and Oetker Ventures, joined by arc investors, Green Generation Fund, AgriFoodTech Venture Alliance, Astanor Ventures and XAnge.

The structure is the notable part. Edition 8 flagged MATR Foods' €40M round — half equity, half European Investment Bank project debt — as a marker that a fermentation company had crossed from "venture-stage" to "infrastructure-stage." Planetary's new round, equity plus a credit facility, is the second company in this map to blend debt and equity instruments. One data point was an anecdote; two is the beginning of a pattern: as European fermentation companies scale past their first plant, lenders — not just venture funds — are becoming part of the cap table.

On the architecture × geography matrix, Planetary sits in the licensing/BioBlocks cell — Switzerland, co-located with a Schweizer Zucker sugar mill. Its financing structure is now drifting toward the same "infrastructure-stage" signature as MATR's solid-state-fermentation cell. That suggests the matrix's architecture axis may itself have a maturity dimension: not just what a company makes and how, but whether its financing has started to look like a plant operator's rather than a venture-backed platform's.

JULINKA'S ANGLE
Three concrete moves — anchored to this edition's news
1.

Ask two pipeline companies one specific question this week: who could you merge science with, on just your hardest regulatory step? Formo's lead in the casein race did not come from better fermentation science — it came from pooling a 60-scientist team with Those Vegan Cowboys on the GRAS dossier specifically, while staying competitors on product. That is a sourcing-relevant pattern, not a one-off: any Calm/Storm pipeline company facing a GRAS-equivalent gating step (EU novel-food approval, FDA self-affirmation, EFSA dossiers) should be asked, this week and before IFT FIRST Chicago on 13 July, whether a competitor exists it could pre-competitively pool R&D with on that specific step. The companies that answer "yes, and here's who" are showing exactly the kind of strategic clarity Calm/Storm should be selecting for.

2.

Reach the nature-finance contact through Re:Invest in Nature, not the policy track. The Paris workshop debrief contact has been carried since Edition 3 and reframed in Edition 8 around a sharper question — what should a nature credit be allowed to fund, given the Committee of the Regions' "supplement not substitute" statement. Re:Invest in Nature, running this week as part of EU Green Week, puts JPMorgan and Rabobank in a room with 70 nature-tech founders — investors who need an answer to that exact question for their own diligence. That is a faster, more relevant route to a useful contact than the policy track this brief has pointed toward for two editions. Send one outreach message this week, framed as an investor-to-investor question, not a policy question.

3.

Pick one Art Basel access point now — First Choice (5 days, gated) or the Gold Awards commissions (open now) — and write down which, before the choice gets made for you. First Choice now has an exact window: Tuesday 11am–4pm, by invitation. The Gold Awards commissions — including Mahama's agricultural-trade-infrastructure piece, the most direct bridge to Calm/Storm's food-systems mandate — are public and open today. Both are legitimate; deciding which one matters more to this week's priorities, in advance, is the difference between a plan and a reaction to whatever invitation does or doesn't arrive.
