From ALDI Suisse's Shelves to a Global Licensing Platform: How Planetary Built the Bioeconomy Infrastructure Play

Planetary SA, the Swiss fermentation company, has closed a CHF 22 million Series A and launched its mycoprotein filet at ALDI Suisse at price parity with conventional chicken. It is the most complete proof-of-concept for the agrifoodtech thesis that capital is now repricing: deeptech with real B2B traction, not consumer brand ambition.

In April 2026, Planetary SA — headquartered in Ecublens, a ten-minute train ride from ETH Zurich's main campus — closed a CHF 16 million equity Series A supplemented by CHF 6 million in credit, bringing total funding to approximately CHF 32 million ($41 million). The lead investors were Radikal Capital and Oetker Ventures. Strategic co-investors included Royal Cosun (a Dutch agricultural cooperative), Arc Investors, Green Generation Fund, AgriFoodTech Venture Alliance, and existing backers Astanor Ventures and XAnge.

What Planetary builds is not a consumer food brand. It is a full-stack fermentation infrastructure and licensing platform: bioprocess design, industrial scale-up, and manufacturing via its proprietary BioBlocks system, which the company lists on the WIPO GREEN database of environmentally sound technologies. The licensing model is the strategic insight. Rather than competing in crowded consumer alternative-protein markets, Planetary sells its technology to agro-industrial players — particularly sugar companies — enabling them to convert low-value processing side streams into high-value proteins, fibres, and enzymes. The target: ultra-low-cost mycoprotein production below $1 per kilogram, achieved through partnerships in sucrose-rich, protein-deficient geographies such as India.

The ALDI Suisse launch is the proof-of-concept that matters. Planetary's new vegan mycoprotein filet launched across ALDI Suisse at price parity with conventional chicken — not at a premium, not with an environmental surcharge, but at the same shelf price as the standard product it displaces. This is the market signal that the alternative protein sector has been reaching for since the Beyond Meat IPO in 2019: cost parity achieved not through subsidy or loss-leader pricing but through a genuinely lower-cost production architecture. Biomass fermentation at industrial scale, using upcycled sugar side streams, structurally undercuts conventional livestock protein on input costs when the feedstock is essentially a processing waste product.

The investor mix tells the story. Oetker Ventures (the strategic arm of Dr. August Oetker KG, the German food conglomerate) and Royal Cosun (a sugar cooperative processing 8 million tonnes of sugar beet annually) are not financial-only investors — they are the distribution and supply-chain partners embedded in the cap table. When Royal Cosun invests in a company that converts sugar side streams into mycoprotein, it is simultaneously creating a new revenue line for its own processing waste. This is the deal structure that works in agrifoodtech in 2026: strategic alignment between the investor's existing business and the startup's technology, not just financial return.

For Julinka, three things about Planetary are directly relevant. First, the science: biomass fermentation (growing mycelium — fungal protein — on a sugar substrate) is a well-understood process at lab scale; Planetary's contribution is the industrial-scale bioprocess engineering that makes it economically viable. An ETH-trained food scientist can evaluate that engineering claim at a level that a generalist investor cannot. Second, the deal structure: the licensing model separates technology value from brand risk — Planetary does not need consumers to prefer its brand, only agro-industrial partners to adopt its process. Third, the geography: this is a Zurich-area company, in Julinka's network proximity, running an active expansion with a Series A closed in April. Calm/Storm's nutrition-tech mandate was the frame; Planetary is the archetype.

EU Green Week Opens on Nature Investment — Expert Group Hits Midpoint

EU Green Week 2026 opened June 3 with an explicit focus on nature investment and the EU's acceleration toward a nature-positive economy. The Commission's nature-credits expert group — established to co-develop certification methodologies and governance principles — is at its midpoint: first results are due in 2026. The Commission confirmed that mid-2026 actions include inviting the expert group to provide expertise on criteria and methodologies for nature credit markets.

The context is stark. The EU's biodiversity financing gap runs to €65 billion per year. Nature credits — defined as quantifiable, fungible units for verified biodiversity outcomes that can be registered, pooled, banked, and traded — are the Commission's primary vehicle for mobilising private capital to close that gap. The pilot project (running 2025–2027) will produce the first credible nature-credit issuances. COP17 CBD in Yerevan, October 2026, is the next global forcing function: the Convention on Biological Diversity's 17th Conference of the Parties will set global biodiversity finance commitments that will either validate or constrain the EU's market-building approach. As introduced in Edition 1, the biodiversity credit market is projected to reach $2bn by 2030 and $69bn by 2050, with the WEF piloting a model that routes ≥75% of credit value to land stewards.

Fermentation Funding Paradox: Down 43% Overall, But the Survivors Have B2B Traction

The headline data from GFI's 2026 sector report is apparently bearish: plant-based investment rose 39% to $450 million in 2025, fermentation fell 43% to $357 million, and cultivated protein dropped 48% to $74 million. But the Planetary and Formo rounds tell a more precise story. Formo secured $36 million in venture debt for biomass fermentation (koji) and precision fermentation (casein); Planetary closed CHF 22 million with strategic investors already embedded in the supply chain. Both companies share a structural feature: they sell to industrial partners, not consumers. The funding paradox is not that fermentation is in decline — it is that consumer-facing fermentation brands are in decline while B2B platform plays are still fundable. This distinction is exactly the analytical lens that Julinka's food-science background makes accessible and that generalist VCs miss.

From Lab to ALDI: How Planetary Built the Infrastructure Play for the Fermentation Economy

In a sector where consumer-facing alternative-protein brands have struggled to achieve profitability, Planetary SA chose a different path. Understanding why that path works — and what it requires technically — is the kind of diligence advantage that a food scientist turned investor has and that a generalist does not.

The fermentation economy has a scaling problem that is not primarily about science. The science of biomass fermentation — growing fungal protein (mycoprotein, mycelium) on a carbohydrate substrate — has been understood at laboratory and pilot scale for decades. Quorn, the UK mycoprotein brand, has been producing Fusarium venenatum protein commercially since the 1980s. The problem is cost: at small-scale production, mycoprotein costs $10–25 per kilogram, which is 10–25 times the cost of conventional chicken breast. At industrial scale with cheap feedstocks, the cost floor drops dramatically. Planetary is building the industrial scale and the cheap feedstock simultaneously.

The BioBlocks System — What It Actually Is

BioBlocks is Planetary's proprietary industrial fermentation platform. The key engineering choices that determine its economics are: (1) vessel design — Planetary uses airlift bioreactors rather than stirred-tank reactors, which reduces energy consumption and shear stress on the fungal mycelium; (2) strain selection — the company works with mycelium strains optimised for growth on sugar-rich, nitrogen-poor substrates (sugar beet molasses, sugar cane bagasse), meaning it does not require the expensive amino acid supplementation that some fermentation processes need; (3) downstream processing — mycoprotein extraction from the fermentation broth requires filtration, washing, and texturisation steps that are energy-intensive; Planetary's BioBlocks system bundles these into a modular unit that can be retrofitted into existing agro-industrial facilities rather than built greenfield.

The WIPO GREEN listing reflects genuine environmental advantage: the feedstocks are agricultural processing side streams (sugar beet molasses, cane bagasse, and other sucrose-rich residues) that would otherwise be low-value waste products. Converting them into protein with a net positive carbon balance — without the land use or water consumption of conventional animal protein — is the environmental claim. Whether that claim survives full lifecycle assessment at commercial scale is the open scientific question.

The Licensing Model — Why It Works When Consumer Brands Don't

The conventional agrifoodtech startup model is to build a consumer brand: Beyond Meat, Oatly, Quorn. These companies bear brand-building costs, retail slotting fees, consumer marketing spend, and the full risk of consumer adoption. In 2022–2024, the market discovered that consumer alternative-protein adoption is slower, more price-sensitive, and more volatile than the sector expected. Valuations collapsed. Funding dried up.

Planetary's licensing model transfers all consumer risk to its industrial partners. Royal Cosun, the Dutch agricultural cooperative, processes approximately 8 million tonnes of sugar beet annually — producing significant volumes of beet molasses as a processing side stream. By licensing Planetary's BioBlocks technology, Royal Cosun converts that side stream into mycoprotein: an existing waste product becomes a premium ingredient. Cosun does not need consumers to prefer the "Planetary" brand; it needs Cosun-branded products containing mycoprotein ingredient to find shelf space. Planetary collects a technology licensing fee on each tonne of production, without carrying the retail risk.

The sub-$1/kg target in protein-deficient geographies is the long-run strategic play. In India, where the sugar industry produces substantial molasses side streams and where plant-based protein is culturally embedded in the diet, a $1/kg mycoprotein production cost makes the ingredient competitive with lentils — the continent's dominant protein source — without the land use of lentil cultivation. Planetary is not trying to sell a product to Indian consumers. It is trying to license technology to Indian sugar cooperatives to produce a protein ingredient for the Indian food industry. That market is measured in hundreds of millions of people, not in the ALDI Suisse footprint.

The ALDI Suisse Launch — Why Price Parity Matters

Conventional chicken breast in Swiss supermarkets retails at approximately CHF 18–22 per kilogram. Alternative protein products have historically retailed at CHF 30–45 per kilogram — a 50–100% premium that consumer research consistently shows is the primary adoption barrier. Planetary's mycoprotein filet launching at price parity with conventional chicken at ALDI Suisse means one of two things: either Planetary has achieved a production cost floor that genuinely supports parity pricing at scale, or ALDI Suisse is accepting lower margins on the product as a strategic portfolio decision. Either interpretation is significant. If the former, it validates the industrial-scale fermentation economics. If the latter, it signals that a major European discounter considers alternative protein important enough to subsidise its launch — which is itself a market-shaping signal.

What Julinka Should Diligence — The Specific Questions

Three technical questions determine whether Planetary's thesis is as strong as the Series A implies. First: what is the actual mycoprotein yield (grams of protein per litre of fermentation broth per hour) that Planetary achieves at current scale, and how does it compare to the published literature for airlift bioreactor mycoprotein production? Second: what is the downstream processing energy cost per kilogram of finished mycoprotein, and has this been independently verified in a lifecycle assessment? Third: what are the licensing terms (royalty percentage, minimum volume commitments, technology transfer scope) that Royal Cosun and other partners have accepted — these reveal how much of the economic upside Planetary captures vs. transfers to its industrial partners.

A food scientist who has spent three years at Migros — watching how a major food retailer evaluates ingredient suppliers, certifies novel food technologies, and manages supply-chain risk — can ask these questions from a position of operational credibility. That is the diligence advantage that translates a food-science background into investment edge. Not reading about fermentation yields; understanding what a yield number means for a production economics model and what questions it raises about a manufacturer's cost claims.
