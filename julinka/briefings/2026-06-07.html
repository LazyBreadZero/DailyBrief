Pacifico Biolabs Raises €7M to Brew Protein in Germany's Idle Beer Tanks — A Third Fermentation Architecture Enters the Field

Sources: EU-Startups 9/10  ·  Tech Funding News 8/10  ·  Green Queen 8/10  ·  Tech.eu 8/10

Pacifico Biolabs, founded in Berlin in 2022 by Zac Austin and Washington Logroño, has closed a €7 million Series A to scale a proposition that sounds almost too simple to be venture-backable: grow mycelium protein directly inside the fermentation tanks that Europe's breweries are no longer using. The round — backed by Stray Dog Capital, TGFS, Sprout & About Ventures, Simon Capital, FoodLabs, and a regional brewery partner — will fund production of up to 200 tonnes per month, anchored at a facility in Saxony, with commercial launches planned across the DACH region and Nordic Europe.

The mechanism is the story. As beer consumption falls across Europe, breweries are sitting on idle, food-grade fermentation capacity — stainless steel tanks built to exacting hygienic standards, with services, cooling, and logistics already in place. Pacifico's mycelium fermentation process runs in those tanks largely unmodified, eliminating the need for the purpose-built bioreactors that have made biomass fermentation so capital-intensive for nearly every other entrant in the category. The company is targeting cost parity with conventional meat from day one — not after a decade of scale-up capital.

Editions 2 through 4 established a single thesis with two data points: B2B fermentation infrastructure — not consumer brands, not cultivated meat — is what the current alt-protein capital cycle is willing to fund, and the Netherlands (Planetary, The Protein Brewery) is its geographic anchor. Pacifico Biolabs is a third data point, and it complicates the thesis productively. It is German, not Dutch. And its capital efficiency comes not from a novel organism or a licensing structure, but from refusing to build anything new at all. The thesis was "infrastructure." Pacifico has revealed that infrastructure does not have to be purpose-built — it can be repurposed. That is a structurally different, and arguably more scalable, answer to the same capital question.

For Calm/Storm, the relevant comparison is not Pacifico versus Protein Brewery on production economics — both will likely reach cost parity on different timelines. It is Pacifico versus Protein Brewery on capital intensity per unit of new capacity brought online. The Protein Brewery's Mijkenbroek facility and Planetary's BioBlocks licensees both require either owned production assets or partner balance sheets willing to absorb conversion costs. Pacifico's model requires neither: it asks a brewery with declining utilisation to rent it tank-time it would otherwise write off. That is a sale that makes itself, in a market — European brewing — that is structurally shrinking and looking for exactly this kind of revenue.

The product itself — a neutral-flavoured, naturally meat-structured mycelium protein positioned as a private-label and food-manufacturer ingredient — is not the differentiator. The differentiator is that Pacifico has found a way to make Europe's industrial overcapacity into its raw material. That is precisely the kind of structural insight — locating value in an adjacent industry's decline — that a generalist fund will miss and a food scientist with manufacturing literacy will not.

The Third Architecture — What Pacifico Biolabs Reveals About the Real Constraint on Fermentation Capital

Edition 5  ·  7 June 2026  ·  Approx. 1,050 words

Two Architectures Were Never the Whole Story

Edition 4's deep read drew a clean line between two fermentation business models: Planetary's BioBlocks, which licenses a fermentation process to agro-industrial partners who already own the side-streams and the balance sheets to scale; and The Protein Brewery's Fermotein, which builds a branded ingredient inside a company-owned facility and sells it into formulation supply chains. Both models, the argument went, had passed the market's current test — credible B2B traction — and the difference between them was a question of which buyer's specification sheet they were built to satisfy.

That framing was correct as far as it went. But it implicitly treated capital intensity as a fixed cost that each architecture simply allocates differently — Planetary pushes it onto licensees, The Protein Brewery absorbs it directly. Pacifico Biolabs' €7 million Series A breaks that assumption. It does not allocate the cost of fermentation infrastructure differently. It removes it. And in doing so, it reveals that the real constraint the entire category has been solving for was never "which organism" or "which buyer." It was always: who pays to build the tanks?

The Insight Hiding in a Declining Industry

Pacifico's mechanism is structurally elegant precisely because it is not novel science. The company grows mycelium protein in standard brewery fermentation tanks — the same stainless-steel, food-grade, climate-controlled vessels that have brewed European beer for a century. What has changed is not the tank. It is the utilisation rate. As beer and alcohol consumption falls across the continent, breweries — particularly mid-sized regional ones, the kind with strong local supply relationships but thinning margins — are left holding expensive, well-maintained fermentation capacity they increasingly cannot justify running at full tilt.

Pacifico's founders, Zac Austin and Washington Logroño, appear to have asked a question that is obvious only in retrospect: what if the constraint on biomass fermentation was never the biology, but the assumption that you had to build new capacity to practise it? A brewery's idle tank-time is, from the brewery's perspective, a sunk cost generating nothing. From Pacifico's perspective, it is exactly the asset class — food-grade, hygienically certified, geographically distributed, already paid for — that every other fermentation company has had to either build from scratch (Fermotein) or convince a partner to build (BioBlocks). Pacifico's Series A round itself encodes this logic: one of its backers is described simply as "a regional brewery partner." The company is not just buying tank-time. It is recruiting the supply side of its own infrastructure model as an investor.

The Diligence Questions This Architecture Raises

A generalist investor will price Pacifico on its funding size, its tonnage target, and its DACH-and-Nordic go-to-market — all reasonable, all visible in the press release. A food scientist's diligence runs deeper, into the places where "repurposed infrastructure" becomes either a durable moat or a quietly fatal flaw. First: consistency. Brewery tanks were engineered for a single, well-characterised fermentation process running at predictable parameters. Mycelium fermentation has different temperature, pH, aeration and contamination-risk profiles. How much retrofitting does each tank actually require, and does that retrofit cost scale linearly as Pacifico adds brewery partners — or does it erode the capital-efficiency argument as it grows? Second: supply security. A brewery's "idle" capacity is not permanently idle — seasonal demand, special releases, and the brewery's own recovery plans could reclaim that tank-time with little notice. What contractual structure protects Pacifico's production calendar against a partner brewery's good year? Third: batch-to-batch variability. Different breweries' tanks will have different histories, different residual microbiomes, different maintenance standards. Standardising a food ingredient's amino acid and structural profile across a distributed, heterogeneous production base is a quality-control problem that centralised facilities like Mijkenbroek do not face — and it is exactly the kind of problem that determines whether an ingredient can ever earn a tier-one food manufacturer's long-term supply agreement.

Why This Changes Where Calm/Storm Should Be Looking

If Pacifico's thesis holds — and the early signal, a brewery itself choosing to invest rather than simply licensing tank-time, suggests its closest industry observers believe it will — then the operative question for sourcing is no longer "which fermentation company has the best organism or the strongest balance sheet." It becomes: which other European industries are sitting on idle, food-grade, underutilised infrastructure that a sufficiently clever biology company could rent rather than replace? Dairies with falling raw-milk intake. Distilleries with seasonal-only production cycles. Even large-scale bakeries with overnight-idle thermal capacity. Each of these is a version of the same arbitrage Pacifico has found in brewing — declining utilisation in a mature industrial category, repurposable by a company that brings the biology and asks for nothing but tank-time.

This reframes the sourcing thesis Calm/Storm has been building since Edition 1. The question was never simply "which fermentation companies." It is "which companies have figured out that Europe's industrial overcapacity is itself an investable asset" — and Pacifico Biolabs is the first to make that argument concrete, fundable, and, if the diligence questions above resolve well, exportable to categories well beyond beer.

"BioBlocks asked who should own the risk of scaling. Fermotein asked who should own the brand. Pacifico asked a more uncomfortable question: does anyone need to own anything new at all? That is the question that should worry every fermentation company that has already raised on the assumption that they do."
