Standing Ovation Raises $34M to Brew Casein from the Cheese Industry's Own Waste — and France Enters the Fermentation Map

Sources: AgFunderNews 9/10  ·  Pulse2 7/10  ·  DairyReporter 7/10

Standing Ovation, founded in Paris in 2020 by Romain Chayot and Frédéric Paques, has closed a €25 million ($28.5M) Series B — plus a further €5 million ($5.7M) in non-dilutive support — to scale production of dairy proteins made not by cows but by microbes engineered to secrete casein, the protein that gives cheese its stretch and milk its body. The round was co-led by Bpifrance's Ecotechnologies 2 fund and Crédit Mutuel Innovation, with Astanor Ventures, Bel Group, Seventure Partners, Danone Ventures and Big Idea Ventures all returning from earlier rounds.

The detail that matters is not the casein. It is what Standing Ovation feeds its microbes. Almost every precision-fermentation competitor runs its organisms on dextrose — a clean, purpose-bought sugar feedstock that is also one of the category's largest recurring costs. Standing Ovation instead uses acid whey: the sour, environmentally troublesome liquid byproduct that every tonne of cottage cheese and Greek yogurt produced in Europe leaves behind, and that the dairy industry has spent decades paying specialist contractors to dispose of safely. Standing Ovation has turned a cost line on someone else's balance sheet into the cheapest possible input on its own.

Editions 4 and 5 built a single thesis in two steps — Europe's fermentation capital cycle funds infrastructure, not consumer brands, and its geographic anchor is the Netherlands, joined three days ago by Germany's Pacifico Biolabs and its repurposed brewery tanks. Standing Ovation does two things to that thesis at once. It adds a third country, France. And it proves the thesis was never really about "infrastructure" — it was about arbitrage on whatever an adjacent industry is already trying to get rid of. Pacifico rents idle tanks. Standing Ovation drinks the runoff. Different asset, same instinct: stop building, start scavenging.

Standing Ovation will not own factories either — it holds exclusive partnerships with Ajinomoto for large-scale production in France and with Tetra Pak for downstream processing, the same "rent the balance sheet" logic that defined the Dutch cluster Edition 5 mapped. CEO Yvan Chardonnens is candid that casein is the harder molecule: most competitors chased beta-lactoglobulin, the whey protein widely seen as the "easier" win, leaving casein's tougher structural-engineering problem — and the patent protection that comes with solving it — comparatively open. The company is targeting an FDA "no-questions" GRAS letter by the end of 2026 and a North American launch in the same window, with an EFSA novel-food submission aimed at EU approval by late 2027 — the identical "America first, Europe on a slower clock" choreography Edition 5 flagged in Fermotein's regulatory path, now confirmed as a pattern rather than a single company's strategy.

For Calm/Storm, the product list is the tell: protein bars, coffee foam, nutraceuticals, ice cream — the exact "clinical nutrition ingredient" wedge Edition 4 identified as the category's real commercial opening, as distinct from the consumer-brand ambitions that burned the first wave of alt-protein capital. Standing Ovation is not trying to convince anyone to love a new kind of milk. It is trying to get its protein into formulations that food manufacturers already buy, at a cost basis no dextrose-fed competitor can match.

The Waste-Stream Thesis — What Three Companies in Three Countries Reveal About Where Fermentation Capital Actually Wants to Go

Edition 6  ·  8 June 2026  ·  Approx. 1,050 words

Three Raises, Read Side by Side

Lay the last eleven days' fermentation-and-protein news end to end and a pattern appears that no single press release could have shown on its own. On May 27, Berlin's Pacifico Biolabs raised €7 million to grow mycelium protein inside the idle fermentation tanks of German breweries that no longer run them at capacity. In February, UK-based The Bland Company quietly closed a £2 million pre-seed to convert the byproducts of rice and pasta production — streams that agricultural processors currently pay to dispose of — into soluble, egg-replacing plant proteins. And this morning, Paris's Standing Ovation announced a $34 million raise built on a single, almost provocative design choice: feed its casein-producing microbes not on bought sugar, but on the acid whey that Europe's cottage-cheese and yogurt makers treat as an expensive disposal problem.

Three companies. Three countries — Germany, the United Kingdom, France. Three entirely different biological mechanisms — biomass fermentation, enzymatic processing, precision (microbial) fermentation. And one identical structural insight, arrived at independently: the cheapest, most defensible input available to a food-biology company is not a novel ingredient bought on the open market. It is whatever an adjacent, often-declining industry is already trying to get rid of.

Edition 5 Asked the Question; June Has Already Started Answering It

This is not a retrospective pattern stitched together for convenience. Edition 5's deep read on Pacifico Biolabs ended by posing a direct, forward-looking question: which other European industries are sitting on idle, food-grade, underutilised assets that a sufficiently clever biology company could rent rather than replace? It named dairies with falling raw-milk intake and distilleries with seasonal-only production cycles as plausible candidates — educated guesses about where the next data point might come from.

The guess about dairies was, it turns out, not speculative enough. Standing Ovation is not merely targeting a dairy's spare capacity — it is consuming a dairy's literal waste output, at the molecular level, as its primary growth medium. And The Bland Company supplies a second confirmation from a completely different direction: agricultural processing, not industrial fermentation infrastructure at all. The question Edition 5 asked rhetorically has been answered, twice, independently, inside a fortnight — which means it was never a rhetorical question. It was a prediction. And it came true faster than the prediction's author expected.

Why "Waste Stream" Is a Better Frame Than "Infrastructure"

Edition 5 organised its thesis around the word "infrastructure" — fixed industrial assets, like fermentation tanks, that a company could rent instead of building. That word was correct for Pacifico, but it is already too narrow for what June has revealed. Acid whey is not infrastructure; it is a liquid byproduct with a negative cost basis. Rice-husk and pasta-water residues are not infrastructure either; they are agricultural waste streams with their own logistics, seasonality and contamination profiles. What unites all three companies is not that they rent physical assets. It is that they have each identified a flow — of tank-time, of liquid byproduct, of agricultural residue — that an adjacent industry currently treats as a cost to be minimised, and have built a business model around treating that same flow as a feedstock to be maximised.

This reframing matters because it is dramatically more expansive. "Idle infrastructure" describes a narrow, identifiable set of physical assets — tanks, factories, pipelines — that a sourcing team could plausibly enumerate. "Waste stream" describes almost anything: liquids, solids, gases, surplus capacity, seasonal gluts, byproducts, off-spec batches, anything that shows up on an income statement as a disposal cost rather than a revenue line. The addressable universe of "companies that could plausibly run this playbook" did not grow by a few names this week. It grew by an order of magnitude — and became, for the first time, genuinely mappable as a category rather than describable only by example.

The Diligence Question That Connects All Three

A pattern this clean should also raise a single, shared concern, because each of these three companies has built its cost advantage on a foundation it does not control: the continued existence, at scale, of someone else's industrial decline. Pacifico's tank-time depends on European breweries continuing to run under capacity — a trend that is currently true, but is also, by definition, a trend that describes an industry contracting toward some eventual floor. Standing Ovation's acid whey depends on Europe continuing to produce cottage cheese and Greek yogurt at volumes that generate disposal-grade surplus; a structural shift in dairy consumption, in either direction, changes the input math. The Bland Company's rice and pasta residues depend on the continuity, geography and processing methods of two specific agricultural supply chains an ocean away from its own operations.

In each case, the moat and the risk are the same fact, viewed from two angles. Building a company on an adjacent industry's waste is brilliant precisely because that waste is, for now, abundant and nearly free. But "for now" is doing real work in that sentence. The diligence question every one of these companies should be asked — and that a generalist investor is unlikely to think to ask — is not "how good is your biology," but "what happens to your unit economics in the scenario where your feedstock industry either recovers, or finishes declining"? Both endpoints remove the arbitrage. Only the company that has planned for both deserves to be called structurally, rather than opportunistically, advantaged.

What This Means for Where Calm/Storm Should Look Next

If the pattern is real — and three independent confirmations inside a fortnight is about as real as patterns get this early — then the operative sourcing question is no longer "which fermentation company has the best organism." It is: which European industries are visibly shrinking, seasonal, or generating costly byproducts at scale, and has anyone yet built a biology company to sit at the end of that industry's drain pipe? Wineries discarding pomace by the tonne each autumn. Sugar-beet processors with seasonal pulp surpluses. Leather tanneries and paper mills with chemically complex waste streams that are expensive, not impossible, to valorise. Bakeries with overnight-idle thermal capacity and stale-product write-offs. Each is a candidate "drain pipe" — and each is now, for the first time, a search heuristic rather than a guess.

That is the shift this edition represents: from "track named milestones in a known cluster," which Edition 4 declared the right discipline three editions ago, to "use the cluster's emerging logic to predict where the next cluster will form." A food scientist who can read an industrial decline curve and ask "what does this industry throw away, and could a microbe eat it" is not doing the same job as a generalist scanning funding databases for the word "fermentation." It is a narrower question, asked of a much larger universe — and that combination, more than any single company on this page, is the actual edge.

"Pacifico asked what a brewery no longer needed. Standing Ovation asked what a dairy pours away each morning. The Bland Company asked what a rice mill leaves on the floor. None of them asked the question a generalist would ask first — 'what is the best biology?' They all asked a better one: 'whose decline can I turn into my supply chain?'"
