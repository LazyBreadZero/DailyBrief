Today’s Lead · Food Regulation

South Korea’s FoodTech law creates the regulatory sandbox the EU just refused to build

South Korea published its “1st Basic Plan for Fostering the FoodTech Industry” on 6 July — the first dedicated statutory framework for FoodTech anywhere. The plan commits 81 billion KRW (approximately €56 million) in 2026, rising to 100 billion KRW by 2027, across R&D, commercialisation infrastructure, and a regulatory sandbox. The sandbox is the structural news: it explicitly permits the upcycling of food processing byproducts into novel food ingredients under regulator-observed conditions — the same iterative, pre-dossier mechanism the EU Biotech Act I introduced in December 2025 and then, under GFI and EuropaBio pressure, was found to have excluded novel foods from entirely. Korea MAFRA 9/10 AgFunderNews 7/10

The contrast with the EU is precise, not rhetorical. The EU Biotech Act I expanded pre-submission EFSA technical guidance — a genuine improvement — but the sandbox mechanism itself, which allows iterative product testing before formal dossier commitment, does not apply to novel foods. Biotech Act II, expected Q3 2026, is the designated correction window; GFI and EuropaBio have named novel-food sandbox inclusion as the specific ask. Korea has now built the instrument Brussels has not. A precision fermentation company or waste-stream ingredient startup that can service a Korean retail or ingredient buyer today faces a materially shorter regulatory path than a European-only commercialisation sequence.

Korea’s strategic intent reads clearly in the plan’s structure: FoodTech is treated as an export-ready industrial category, not a domestic food-safety matter. The sandbox covers cultivated meat, precision fermentation, and upcycled ingredients — the three production pathways at the centre of European alt-protein funding — and pairs them with dedicated commercialisation support, including export facilitation. That is not an accident: K-food’s global expansion over the past decade was built on the same state-as-commercial-accelerator logic, applied first to hallyu and then to functional food exports. FoodTech is the next category in that sequence.

Julinka’s Angle
1 Before Biotech Act II publishes (Q3 2026), ask each Calm/Storm pipeline company whose EU commercialisation thesis rests on a waste-stream or upcycling ingredient pathway one question: does your timeline assume the EU sandbox will cover you? The answer tells you whether the company is running on a regulation that exists (EU Biotech Act I expanded EFSA guidance — real, slower) or one that is still being negotiated (Biotech Act II sandbox inclusion — named as the correction but not yet law). If the answer is “yes, we need the sandbox,” Korea’s statutory framework is now a named alternative jurisdiction for a commercial pilot. That does not require a full Korea market entry: it requires identifying one Korean ingredient buyer or distributor already operating in the EU food-manufacturing supply chain. Pacifico, Standing Ovation, The Bland Company, and MOA Foodtech are the four companies in the waste-stream cluster worth checking first.
2 Formo’s IFT FIRST commercial debut opens in two days (13–16 July, Chicago, Booth ST81). Before the floor coverage floods on Monday, write the three functional performance numbers that define whether precision-fermented casein is commercially decisive for a dairy-free cheese manufacturer: gel strength at pH 4.6 (acid-curd texture benchmark), micellar fidelity under heat (melt specification for food-service), and slow-digestion kinetics against native bovine casein (satiety claim for functional food positioning). Your ETH fermentation training gives you these benchmarks before the marketing narrative arrives. If Formo’s published or presented data meets all three, the GRAS review is the only remaining barrier; if it meets two, the weaker benchmark names the R&D risk to probe at the booth.
