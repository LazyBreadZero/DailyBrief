The Nature Credits Roadmap Stops Being a Document — IUCN Launches Live Pilots in Croatia, the Netherlands and Poland

IUCN Official 10/10   EU Commission Green Forum 10/10

On 2 June, IUCN convened officials from the European Commission's SG REFORM and DG Environment, plus national authorities from Croatia, the Netherlands and Poland, to launch an EU-funded project exploring nature credits and nature markets in those three countries. The brief is explicit: give national governments the evidence to decide whether, and how, nature credit schemes could finance restoration — feasibility, design and governance, country by country. This is the moment the Nature Credits Roadmap, first flagged in this brief back in Edition 2, stops being a Brussels policy paper and becomes three live national conversations with dates and named institutions attached.

Step back: this thread is now four months and five editions deep, and the shape of it has been a slow narrowing from abstraction to instrument. Edition 2 introduced the European Commission's Nature Credits Roadmap — COM(2025)374 — as a framework to "reward nature-positive action and boost private finance," with an expert group convening through mid-2026. Edition 3 added COP17 Yerevan as the diplomatic backdrop. Editions 7 and 8 brought the Paris nature-positive finance workshop (Biodiversa+ and the Banque de France) and the European Committee of the Regions' "supplement not substitute" statement — the first hint that the roadmap's critics wanted nature credits constrained to topping up, not replacing, public conservation finance. Edition 9's Re:Invest in Nature put 70 nature-tech startups in a room with JPMorgan and Rabobank — the investor-facing complement to the policy track. What none of those five editions had was a government actually deciding whether to build one of these schemes. That is what started on 2 June.

The choice of countries is itself a signal. Croatia, the Netherlands and Poland span three very different starting points: the Netherlands has the EU's densest existing private-finance-for-nature infrastructure (IUCN NL itself, a major land-acquisition-for-nature programme, and the institutional investors — Rabobank prominent among them — who showed up at Re:Invest in Nature); Poland represents a large agricultural economy where a nature credit scheme would have to interact with EU Common Agricultural Policy payments at scale; Croatia is a coastal and biodiversity-rich member state where tourism-linked natural capital is the more obvious asset class. A scheme that can be designed coherently across all three — dense-finance, large-agricultural, coastal-biodiversity — is closer to a template the other 24 member states could adapt than a one-off pilot in a single favourable jurisdiction would be.

For Calm/Storm, this is the first point in the nature-credits thread where "watch the policy" becomes "watch the instrument design." Feasibility-and-design studies generate exactly the kind of stakeholder consultation that produced the WePlanet submission to Biotech Act II (Edition 9) — a structured, time-boxed window in which outside input on scope and eligibility criteria is solicited and weighted. If nature credit eligibility criteria end up shaped to reward, say, regenerative agriculture practices verifiable via remote sensing, or biodiversity outcomes tied to specific crop or fermentation feedstock sourcing, that is a direct line into the kind of agrifoodtech and climate-tech portfolio Calm/Storm is building. The window to have a view on that design is now — not when the Q4 2026 Biotech Act II proposal lands, and not when whichever member state moves first publishes draft legislation.

CONTINUITY NOTE

As introduced in Edition 2 (4 June 2026), the Nature Credits Roadmap was a policy framework with an expert group convening through mid-2026 — at that point, a document with a timetable. Reinforcement: a roadmap becomes investable infrastructure the moment it acquires a named pilot, a named country, and a named convening institution. As of 2 June, the EU nature-credits thread has all three, in triplicate. From here, the question is no longer "will this happen" but "which design choices are still open, and to whom."

"A roadmap is a promise about the future. A pilot in three named countries, with a convening date already in the past, is a promise that has started keeping itself."

Art Basel's Digital Art Sector Gets Its Theme — "The Condition," Curated by Trevor Paglen, Four Days Before First Choice

Art Basel has confirmed the line-up for Zero 10, its digital-art initiative, at its largest presentation yet: 20 exhibitors, co-curated by artist Trevor Paglen (2026 LG Guggenheim Award, MacArthur Fellow) and digital-art strategist Eli Scheinman, under the anchor theme "The Condition" — life in a world saturated by digital imagery, computational systems and AI. That makes Zero 10's preview the lower-friction half of the same day First Choice is the higher-friction half: one event, two access tiers, on the calendar's most-watched date.

"The Condition" is also the first Art Basel programming this brief has covered that names AI and computational systems as its subject rather than its method — a direct overlap with the convergence questions running through Calm/Storm's nutrition and climate-tech thesis (data-driven regenerative agriculture, remote-sensing-verified biodiversity claims, the kind of "computational systems shaping the natural world" territory the nature-credits lead above is moving into). Paglen built his career photographing the infrastructure of surveillance; a curator who thinks that way, applied to a fair Julinka already has reason to attend, is worth twenty minutes regardless of what else is on the calendar that week.

FOOD SCIENCE · REGULATION

Debut and Oterra Pair Up to Take Precision-Fermented Color at Red 40 — a Three-Year Regulatory Clock, Filed Before It Started Ticking

Debut, the precision-fermentation ingredients company, and Oterra, the world's largest natural-colour producer, announced a multi-million-dollar partnership to develop a fermentation-derived alternative to Red 40 — one of the most widely used synthetic red dyes in US food and drink, and a primary target of state-level synthetic-dye bans gathering pace this year. Combining Debut's fermentation strains with Oterra's formulation expertise, the partnership aims to cover the full orange-red-violet shade range, with an FDA approval filing as the explicit near-term goal and commercial scale targeted in roughly three years.

Read against Edition 9's "third axis" — regulatory-pathway stage, tracked separately for US GRAS status and EU Biotech Act II exposure — this is a company filing its regulatory dossier before its commercial product exists, rather than after. Formo's casein (Edition 9) entered active GRAS review for a product already approaching market; Debut and Oterra are sequencing the FDA filing as the first step of a three-year roadmap. Two different postures toward the same axis: race to the regulator with a near-finished product, versus build the regulatory case as the foundation the product is engineered against from day one.

Color additives are a smaller, less capital-intensive category than casein or egg protein, but the FDA pathway is the same machinery — and a three-year filing-to-market runway for a colour ingredient is, in practice, a bet that US synthetic-dye policy keeps moving in the direction it has been moving in 2025–26. Oterra's scale (it already supplies a large share of the world's natural colours) makes this look less like a startup bet and more like an incumbent hedging its Red 40-dependent customer base against a regulatory direction it considers close to settled.

VENTURE · PREVENTIVE HEALTH

Rem3dy Health Raises €16M — Strategic Capital From Suntory and Apollo Hospitals, Aimed at Personalised Health and a Pivot Into Pet Nutrition

Birmingham-based Rem3dy Health — personalised nutrition via 3D-printed supplements — closed a €16M round led by a group of strategic investors: Suntory, Estrella Galicia, Apollo Hospitals and UPSA. The funds back expansion into the US, MENA and India, and a new line into personalised pet-health products. Preventive health is 2026's fastest-growing European healthtech category by funding (+88% year-on-year to $869M, per Galen Growth), and this round is a data point for a sub-pattern within it: strategic corporate investors — a beverage group, a hospital network, a pharma — rather than pure financial VCs, taking board-relevant stakes in personalisation plays. Worth flagging for Calm/Storm's own cap-table composition conversations on personalised-nutrition deals. EU-Startups 8/10

NATURE · FINANCE

The Other Number Behind Today's Lead: Biodiversity Loss Could Add $162bn a Year to Sovereign Borrowing Costs

New analysis circulating ahead of today's lead story puts a number on why governments — not just funds — are now the audience for nature-finance instruments: unchecked biodiversity loss could cost the global economy $2 trillion a year and raise sovereign borrowing costs by a combined $162bn annually, with credit-downgrade risk concentrated in India, China, Indonesia, Bangladesh and Malaysia. The CBD's Astrid Schomaker has been citing this kind of figure in discussions of where nature credits fit in sovereign financing conversations. It is the macro case for why Croatia, the Netherlands and Poland are treating nature-credit feasibility as a finance-ministry question, not only an environment-ministry one. IUCN 10/10
