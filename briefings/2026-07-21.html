Apple kills Vision Air at the component level — a supplier has stopped display work

An Apple supply-chain partner has ended engineering work on the display components intended for Vision Air, Bloomberg reports, The Apple Post 7/10 converting what had been a CEO announcement and an analyst report into a supply-chain fact. Cancellations that reach the component layer do not reverse. The question is no longer whether Vision Air will arrive — it will not, at least not in any recognisable form — but what Apple's post-Vision Pro map actually contains.

The answer: two products, neither of them a headset, on a long horizon. Audio-only smart glasses — display-free, AI voice, camera, no spatial operating system — are targeting late 2027 mass production, running custom low-power silicon derived from Apple Watch architecture rather than the M-series chips in Vision Pro. Waveguide AR with a genuine display sits in 2029 at the earliest. Between now and late 2027 is a gap, and Apple is filling it with nothing: Vision Pro remains on sale at $3,499 with no near-term successor and no clear demand argument. 9to5Mac 8/10 MacRumors 9/10

Vision Pro launched in February 2024 with a promise it was the "beginning" of something. Forty months later, the beginning has ended without a second chapter on schedule. The gap matters structurally. Vision Pro served as a narrative anchor — for journalists, for investors, for the category's broader public intelligibility. When the FT or the Economist wrote about spatial computing, they could point to a device. When Angelo describes the "post-screen human interface," the most vivid demonstration his audience can hold has no credible follow-up product for two years. Samsung Galaxy Glasses, Meta's glasses line, and XREAL Aura are real products in the same direction, but they are less obviously transformative — smaller form factors, less immersive, harder to make visibly compelling to an audience that hasn't worn them.

The practical consequence is a category in a holding pattern. Genuine adoption data, commercial developer incentives, and enterprise use cases are accumulating at the glasses tier — Neuralink's now-26 active trial participants Neuralink 9/10 in BCI, and Samsung's Galaxy XR rolling out in UK enterprise with Knox certification, show adjacent tiers advancing — but the flagship narrative device that could draw non-specialist investment and attention into the category is on a two-year break.

Angelo's Angle

Vision Pro was doing work for Angelo that he may not have fully noticed: it made the category legible to people who would otherwise require a lengthy explanation. When a CFO or a policy lead asked "why does this matter?", the answer was partly "because Apple bet $3,499 that it does." That argument is now two years away from having a successor. The glasses that will exist in that gap — Samsung's, Meta's, Apple's own eventual audio frame — are more modest and require a more specific argument.

What is the one-sentence claim about smart glasses — not XR, not spatial computing, not the "post-screen interface," but this specific category of $300–$500 audio-and-camera frames — that is concrete enough to defend in a conversation with a CFO who has never worn a pair? Not what they will eventually become. What they can demonstrably do for a named decision-making problem, right now.
