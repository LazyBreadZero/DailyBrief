Neuralink Completes Its First Surgery Through the Intact Dura

In May 2026, Neuralink performed its first BCI implantation through an intact dura mater at Toronto Western Hospital, part of the CAN-PRIME study led by neurosurgeon Andres Lozano. The device’s electrode threads — thinner than a human hair — were threaded through the brain’s protective membrane using a redesigned precision needle, with the surgical robot actively mapping blood vessels to avoid them as it placed electrodes into cortex. The patient was controlling a cursor within an hour of surgery. Neuralink announced the milestone on 30 June. Neuralink 9/10 Road to VR 7/10

The dura mater is a leathery membrane more than ten times the thickness of Neuralink’s polyimide threads, and the durectomy — opening or removing part of it — had been the most manually demanding step in Neuralink’s implantation procedure since its first human surgery in January 2024. Eliminating the durectomy reduces infection risk, shortens recovery time, and makes the procedure more compatible with the robotic automation Neuralink committed to for 2026. Elon Musk described the advance as “a real path to scaling” — from the roughly twelve participants implanted across commercial BCI trials industry-wide toward the thousands the company has publicly targeted. Musk/X 9/10

The comparison with competing architectures matters. Science Corp’s PRIMA retinal implant and Synchron’s endovascular approach avoid the durectomy entirely by design; their lead on this specific axis was architectural from the start. Neuralink has now closed that particular gap incrementally. The surgical question has an answer. The support question does not: a Nature Medicine paper published in June documented 3,800 hours of home BCI use across two years without a researcher in the room — but periodic software updates from the research team were still required. Surgery can be automated with a robot. What follows surgery cannot be automated the same way.

The commercial implication is specific. High-volume robotic implantation removes the most variable human step in the surgical procedure. Scaling from a dozen patients to a thousand requires an equivalent reduction in variability on the support side — who recalibrates the decoder if it drifts, who the patient contacts when the interface underperforms, who holds liability for a firmware update that goes wrong. None of the three commercial BCI companies has answered that question in public documentation that has been tested at scale. Neuralink’s robot can now place electrodes reliably. Whether it can also support patients reliably once the operating theatre empties is the question the next thousand implants will answer before any regulator does. Glitchwire 7/10

Angelo’s Angle

Neuralink’s transdural step closes the surgical automation gap, and the logic is credible: remove the most manual step, and the procedure scales more cleanly through a robot. The question it does not close is on the other side of the operating theatre. The BrainGate2 three-year home run still required periodic software updates from the research team, even without a researcher in the room. Surgery scales through automation; patient support scales through infrastructure, liability coverage, and people. Name one commercial BCI company that has built the latter rather than assumed a hospital partner handles it, and state what specifically they have built.
