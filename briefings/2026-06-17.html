XREAL Opens Reservations for Android XR’s First Hardware. Most of Europe Waits Until Wave Two.

XREAL opened reservations on 16 June for AURA, the first consumer hardware built on Google’s Android XR platform and Qualcomm’s new Snapdragon Reality Elite chipset. A $99 deposit converts into $199 of launch credit; $299 buys a numbered “Founder Priority Pass” guaranteeing day-one delivery, capped at 2,000 units worldwide. XREAL has not disclosed a final retail price. The glasses ship this fall to the US, UK, Japan, Canada and South Korea — Wave 1, in the company’s own language. Continental Europe gets Wave 2, described only as “coming soon after.” PR Newswire / XREAL (9/10)

AURA weighs under 95 grams and carries a 70-degree field of view through optical see-through lenses, running a split-compute design that pairs the Reality Elite engine with XREAL’s own X1S spatial coprocessor. Google’s own recap of the launch calls it, plainly, XREAL’s “first wired XR glasses powered by Android XR” — a tethered product, unlike the standalone Galaxy XR headset Samsung shipped on the same platform in January. Best Buy is the first US retail partner; Chi Xu, XREAL’s chief executive, calls it “a new benchmark for OST XR glasses.” Google (9/10)

The geography is not unique to XREAL. A day earlier, on the same Long Beach stage, Snap opened preorders for Specs — its own standalone AR glasses, $2,195, a 51-degree field of view, four-hour battery — for delivery this fall to exactly three countries: the US, the UK, and France. Two AR product lines, built by two unrelated companies, launching the same season, have between them committed real hardware to Britain and France and nowhere else in Europe. The Android XR Developer Catalyst Programme, whose application window closes June 30, still has not named a European developer cohort either. What had been a single missing announcement is now visible at the developer-access layer and the retail layer at once. TechCrunch (9/10) Android Developers (9/10)

Angelo’s Angle

Two real AR product lines ship this fall, and neither chose Vienna, or Berlin, or Rome, as a first market. You have spent seventeen editions narrating global momentum in this field from a city that will not be able to buy the hardware until wave two. Name one upcoming conversation with a European decision-maker where you can put a device in their hands rather than describe one secondhand — and if you cannot name one this month, ask yourself whether “translator” currently means something different from “narrator.”
