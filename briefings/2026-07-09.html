Synchron's Pivotal Trial Is Enrolling at Four US Sites — and the First FDA PMA for a Permanently Implanted BCI Is Now in Sight

Synchron's COMMAND pivotal trial, funded by a $200 million Series D raised specifically for this purpose in November, is now enrolling patients across four or more US clinical sites — the clearest step yet toward filing the first Pre-Market Approval (PMA) application for a permanently implanted communication brain-computer interface. No permanently implanted BCI aimed at restoring speech or cursor control has ever received full FDA premarket approval. Synchron is first in line to change that.

The device, the Stentrode, is implanted endovascularly — threaded through the jugular vein and positioned on the surface of the motor cortex without opening the skull. That path earned it a 6/6 safety record in the COMMAND early feasibility study, with zero serious adverse events and 100% successful deployment across all six patients. The endovascular approach means a neurosurgeon is not required; a trained interventional cardiologist or vascular surgeon can place it, which is a structural advantage at every hospital that wants to offer the procedure without standing up a full neurosurgical programme.

The pivotal study's primary endpoint is safety, with efficacy data collected as secondary outcomes for communication tasks: point-and-click computer control, browsing, text messaging. Synchron has always scoped itself around communication rather than fine motor restoration — a deliberate choice that simplifies both the decoder problem and the FDA's risk-benefit calculation. The Caltech finding published in late June (Communications Biology) demonstrated that slow, sustained movement needs continuous external input that a standard autonomous decode cannot provide. Synchron's calibrated communication scope sidesteps that constraint, at the cost of a narrower commercial ceiling than what Neuralink is pursuing.

Three companies are approaching the commercial-support inflection point by three different routes. Synchron deploys through hospital partnerships. Neuralink routes maintenance through its app and its R1 surgical robot, targeting high-volume production this year. Precision Neuroscience has partnered with Medtronic for distribution of its thin-film Layer 7 cortical interface. The question all three must eventually answer is the same one the BrainGate2 ALS result made concrete in June: who is responsible for first-line support when the device is operating in someone's home in year two, and who is authorised to adjust the decoder between approved software updates?

PMA filing sequence matters commercially. The first company to file a PMA for a permanently implanted communication BCI does not simply win a regulatory race — it writes the initial pricing code and reimbursement terms that Medicare and commercial insurers anchor to. Subsequent PMA applicants are then benchmarked against that precedent. If Synchron files first on a communication-task indication, it may own the category's commercial definition for longer than its own product lifespan, even if Neuralink's broader capabilities eventually surpass its own.

Angelo’s Angle

Synchron is first in line for the PMA that writes the category's reimbursement rules. Before it files, name the specific Medicare question its hospital-partnership model answers that Neuralink's platform model doesn't — and vice versa. The BrainGate2 result showed home use is feasible for 3,800 hours without a researcher; neither company has publicly stated who the decoder's "authorised adjuster" is once the device is live in a patient's home. That answer is the reimbursement conversation. Which company has actually thought it through?
