TODAY'S LEAD · BRAIN-COMPUTER INTERFACES · CLINICAL

Neuralink Now Has Patients in Three Countries. Restoring Sight Is the Next Implant.

TechCrunch 9/10   Ophthalmology Times 8/10   Neuralink / FDA 10/10   PDP Spectra 7/10

Neuralink has now implanted its N1 device in roughly twenty patients across three jurisdictions — eleven in the United States, seven in the United Kingdom, two in Canada — running its PRIME, CONVOY and CAN-PRIME trials in parallel rather than in sequence. Noland Arbaugh, the first recipient, has logged thousands of hours of continuous use. The device the company has signalled is next is the more consequential one: Blindsight, a visual-cortex implant that restores a crude form of sight to people who have lost it, holds an FDA Breakthrough Device Designation, with a first human implantation expected this year.

Two years ago a brain implant was a single patient in a laboratory. It is now a multi-site clinical programme operating under three regulators at once, and that shift changes the binding question. The constraint is no longer whether the hardware works — Arbaugh's hours settle that for motor control — but whose rules govern it, and how fast. Neuralink chose to spread trials across countries with different approval clocks instead of waiting on any single one. The electrode stopped being the hard part. The regulatory map became it.

A quieter company is further down the same road, by a different route. Max Hodak's Science Corp is not trying to read the brain at all; its PRIMA implant is a two-millimetre photovoltaic chip that sits beneath the retina and is driven by infrared glasses, restoring a measurable form of vision in 38 patients in its published trial. Science raised $230m in March to commercialise it and expects a European CE mark by mid-2026, with Germany — and its early-access pathway for novel devices — the likely first market. Where Neuralink stimulates the visual cortex, Science drives the eye and leaves the brain alone.

For someone holding the whole field at once, the takeaway is not which implant is cleverer. It is that the first commercially approved vision implant is now a near-term event rather than a thesis, and on current timelines it arrives in Europe, on Science's retinal route, before Neuralink's cortical one reaches a single sighted patient. The variable that decides the category is no longer the device. It is which jurisdiction certifies first, and what reimbursement code and surgical protocol it writes when it does.

XR · SMART GLASSES

AWE Opens With the Smart-Glasses Market Splitting Into Three Tiers

AWE USA 2026 opened in Long Beach today under the theme "I, Spatial." The clearest signal from the floor is structural: smart glasses are no longer one category but three. At the bottom, AI camera eyewear with no display, in the Ray-Ban Meta mould. In the middle, tethered AR glasses such as Google and XREAL's Project Aura, capable but dependent on a separate Android XR compute puck. At the top, a fully standalone AR computer.

Snap's Specs occupy that top tier alone: see-through lenses, an on-board operating system in Snap OS, all processing handled on the device, powered by Qualcomm's Snapdragon XR platform, and slated to reach consumers this autumn at roughly $2,500. If Snap ships on time, it is the first true standalone consumer AR glasses from a major company. The price tells you which tier is a product this year and which is still a demonstration.

XR · CONSUMER · CAPITAL

Rec Room's Shutdown Dates the Consumer-VR Winter

Rec Room, the social-VR platform that raised $294m and reached a $3.5bn valuation in 2021, closed permanently on 1 June after failing to reach sustainable profitability. With $100m-plus XR rounds now rare and Reality Labs still deep in losses, capital has rotated out of consumer VR toward exactly the two places this edition's money is moving: AI glasses and clinical neurotech. The headset era it belonged to is being wound down while the glasses-and-implant era is funded.
