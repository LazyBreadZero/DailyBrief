Vision Pro Goes on Ice — Gurman Confirms Apple Has Abandoned the Headset Bet, Hours Before Tim Cook's Last Keynote

Sources: AppleInsider 8/10  ·  UploadVR 8/10  ·  MacRumors 8/10

Hours before Tim Cook walks onstage for the last time as Apple's CEO, the clearest signal yet about what comes after him has already leaked — and it is not about glasses. It is about the headset Apple spent a decade building and two years selling. According to Bloomberg's Mark Gurman, a Vision Pro successor is "in testing, but the category is on ice." Apple staked $3,499 and its industrial-design reputation on launching the spatial-computing headset category in 2024. Eighteen months later, the company has functionally shelved it — not with a press release, but with the quiet, deliberate vagueness Apple reserves for projects it wants to keep alive on paper without committing another cent to ship them.

As tracked in Edition 6 (6 June), incoming CEO John Ternus had already narrowed Apple's roadmap to two smart-glasses paths — display-less AI glasses for 2027, waveguide AR glasses for 2029. Gurman's reporting now fills in the casualties behind that narrowing: Vision Air, the cheaper, lighter Vision Pro alternative, was killed in October 2025; "display glasses" were cut from the roadmap as far back as January 2025; and the Vision Pro line itself — while a successor remains "in testing" — has been frozen rather than formally cancelled.

Step back: this is the second time in three years Apple has retreated from a category it announced with maximal public confidence — the first was the car. A pattern is now visible in daylight: Apple commits to a frontier hardware bet at extraordinary cost, ships a five-figure proof-of-concept to a tiny audience, lets analysts do the work of walking the story back over eighteen months of leaks, and then quietly narrows to the version of the product that fits its actual playbook — a mass-market accessory, priced to attach to an iPhone someone already owns. Vision Pro was never meant to be the destination. It was the most expensive market-research instrument in consumer-electronics history, and the data came back negative on standalone headsets and positive on glasses-as-accessory.

The numbers on what survives that research now have real edges. Gurman places the first AI glasses (codename N50) at "late 2027" — slipped from the late-2026/early-2027 window reported as recently as Edition 6 — with Ming-Chi Kuo projecting mass production from Q2 2027 and 3–5 million units shipped in year one. Price: $299–499, aimed squarely at Meta's Ray-Ban range ($299–379) and just below where Android XR's Warby Parker/Gentle Monster frames are expected to land ($379–499, Edition 6). No display in the first generation — the device works as an iPhone satellite, an AirPods-and-Watch-style accessory, not a standalone computer. The full-AR "Apple Glass," with a waveguide display, is now pushed toward "the decade's end" — effectively 2029–2030.

For anyone building a career on the thesis that the post-screen interface is coming, this is not bad news — it is clarifying news. It tells the market, conclusively, which of two competing visions of spatial computing's near future just won: not "everyone wears an immersive headset," but "everyone wears glasses that quietly extend the phone." That is the same bet Android XR is making with three hardware partners (Edition 7), the bet Meta made with Ray-Ban, and now — after retreating from the opposite bet at a cost running into the billions — the bet Apple is making too. The argument over form factor is no longer contested. The only question left worth writing about is which company's software layer makes glasses-as-accessory indispensable rather than gimmicky — and that is precisely the question Tim Cook starts answering, for the first time in public, in roughly nine hours.

"Vision Pro was never the destination. It was the most expensive market-research exercise in consumer-electronics history — and the data came back against headsets, and for glasses."

Today: Tim Cook's Final Keynote Brings Gemini-Powered Siri — and a visionOS 27 Accessibility Feature Almost No One Is Covering

At 10:00am Pacific today (7:00pm in Vienna), Tim Cook delivers his last keynote as Apple CEO. The marquee announcement, flagged across this brief since Edition 7, is now confirmed in detail: Apple has licensed a custom 1.2-trillion-parameter Gemini model from Google — at roughly $1bn a year — to rebuild Siri around a standalone, chat-style interface with on-screen awareness and cross-app actions. Six operating-system betas ship within hours, including visionOS 27, which pre-briefing coverage describes uniformly as "light on new features."

Buried inside that "light" release is the one visionOS 27 feature that should matter most to Angelo: Wheelchair Control, which repurposes Vision Pro's existing eye-tracking hardware to drive a powered wheelchair. It is a small line in a pre-briefing list — and a striking one. The same eye-tracking stack Apple built to help people navigate a $3,499 spatial-computing toy has just become assistive medical infrastructure, shipped for free, as a software update. That is the BCI-adjacent story hiding inside today's keynote, and almost no outlet previewing it has named it as one.

Three Funerals in Thirty Days: What Spatial, Rec Room, and Horizon Worlds Tell Us About the Category After the Metaverse

Edition 8  ·  8 June 2026  ·  Approx. 1,050 words

A Decade, Three Funerals

This week, three different companies confirmed the end of three different attempts to build the same thing. Spatial — the creator-economy platform behind Animal Company's Wooster Games — announced it is closing Spatial Creator, ending free and paid subscriptions and shutting down 3D world hosting next month. It follows Rec Room, which closed its doors on June 1, and trails not far behind Meta's own Horizon Worlds, which has quietly stopped pretending to be a destination and repositioned itself as something "mostly for mobile users." Three platforms. Three different parent companies, funding histories, and design philosophies. The same conclusion, reached within thirty days of each other: nobody is building a persistent social life inside a headset, and continuing to try has become expensive enough to stop.

A Pattern Older Than Any of Them

Step back, and this pattern long predates Spatial, Rec Room, or Horizon Worlds. Second Life launched in 2003 promising a parallel social internet; it found a few hundred thousand devoted residents and never broke out of that ceiling. Meta — by its own disclosures — spent in excess of $50 billion building Horizon Worlds and the Reality Labs apparatus around it, renamed the entire company in the process, and produced a platform that, by its own 2026 repositioning, works best as something to glance at from a phone. High Fidelity, AltspaceVR, and VRChat's own commercial ambitions each restated the same premise in their turn: that presence — the felt sense of being somewhere, with someone — is the product. Each discovered, at a different scale and a different cost, that presence alone does not generate a business. People do not pay subscriptions for a feeling, advertisers do not buy placements inside one, and creators do not build careers around it. They build around problems solved.

The Question None of Them Answered

What none of these platforms solved — and what almost none of this week's coverage of their closures will name — is governance: the question of what a platform owes the people whose time, attention, behavioural data, and embodied presence it captures. Presence is not a neutral good. A platform that can observe where your eyes go, how your body moves, and who you choose to stand close to has captured something categorically different from one that has merely captured your scroll history. Yet none of Spatial, Rec Room, or Horizon Worlds ever built, marketed, or monetised an answer to what that captured intimacy is worth, who owns it, or what its limits should be. They built worlds. They never wrote a constitution for the worlds they built. Users sensed the absence even when they could not name it — and drifted back to platforms that, whatever their other faults, do not ask them to inhabit a body inside a space someone else owns.

The Migration Nobody Announced

The capital and attention that left consumer social XR did not vanish. It moved, visibly, to exactly the place this brief has tracked all year: enterprise and utility XR. Spatial-computing vendors now sell into manufacturing, logistics, training, and CAD-heavy workflows where the pitch is not "come live here" but "this tool cuts your error rate by a measurable percentage and plugs into the systems you already run." Samsung, XREAL, and — as of this morning — Apple are not building social worlds; they are building glasses that quietly extend a phone. Android XR's entire pitch (Edition 7) is ecosystem distribution into categories that already exist — eyewear, productivity, navigation — not the construction of new social terrain. The industry has, in effect, quietly agreed that "the metaverse" was the wrong noun all along. The right one was always "interface."

Why This Should Worry — and Motivate — Angelo More Than Most

This is where the story stops being a eulogy and starts being a warning. Every company now chasing the interface bet — Apple with its delayed glasses, Android XR with its three hardware partners, Meta with Ray-Ban — is repeating, at smaller individual scale but vastly higher aggregate stakes, exactly the mistake that just killed three social-XR platforms: building the device first and discovering the governance question only after millions of people are already wearing it. A pair of glasses with always-on cameras and microphones, AI-mediated attention, and — eventually — physiological sensing is a more intimate data-capture surface than any headset Spatial or Horizon Worlds ever shipped, and it will reach two orders of magnitude more people, faster, precisely because it looks like a normal accessory rather than a declaration of intent. The platforms that died this month had the luxury of failing in private, at small scale, before their governance question became unavoidable. The glasses generation will not have that luxury. It will hit consumer scale and the governance question at the same moment — and whoever has already written the framework for answering it will not be improvising when the first scandal breaks.

That is the argument for writing the consent-layer essay now — not after AWE, when the framing will already belong to someone else — and for opening it with these three names. Spatial, Rec Room, and Horizon Worlds are not historical curiosities. They are the closing chapter of the device-first era, and the opening exhibit in the case for building governance into the next one before it ships, not after it apologises. Nobody covering this week's shutdown news is making that connection. The person who makes it first, in public, with named companies and a structural argument rather than a eulogy, becomes the person the next wave of device-makers calls before they ship — not the one they call afterward, to explain themselves.

"They built worlds. They never wrote a constitution for the worlds they built — and the next generation of device-makers is about to make the same omission, at a hundred times the scale."
