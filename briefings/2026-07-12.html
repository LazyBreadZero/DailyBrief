Meta Pushed a Firmware Update Killing Modified Ray-Ban Cameras. The FT Reports a Prototype That Does What Those Modifications Did.

On 7 July, Meta distributed an over-the-air firmware update to Ray-Ban Meta smart glasses that targeted a specific class of user modifications: units whose camera hardware had been physically altered to defeat the privacy LED. The LED on Ray-Ban Meta is designed to illuminate when the camera is active; a community of hardware tinkerers had learned to remove or bypass it, allowing capture without the indicator. Meta’s update detected the modification and disabled camera function on those units permanently. 9to5Google 8/10

Two days later, on 9 July, the Financial Times reported that Meta has an internal hardware prototype it calls “super sensing.” As described by people familiar with the project and corroborated by Fortune on 11 July, the device is a glasses-form-factor AI camera eyewear product engineered for always-on environmental capture. The LED is not a privacy safeguard on this prototype; it is not engaged during capture. The disclosure mechanism the Ray-Ban Meta provides — and the one the modders were defeating — is absent from the design rather than removed from a device that otherwise has it. Fortune 9/10

The two events read together are not contradictory: Meta’s enforcement capacity over hardware modifications and its internal product roadmap are unrelated programmes at the company. But the structural relationship between them is worth stating plainly. Meta’s firmware update demonstrates the company can detect physical alterations to its privacy architecture and neutralise them at the software layer. The “super sensing” prototype designs the privacy disclosure layer out of the capture sequence entirely, not as a modification but as the original architecture. The modders who removed the LED were building, without Meta’s authorisation, a device that the company’s own engineers are reportedly building with it.

The enforcement action establishes that Meta treats unauthorised circumvention of its privacy hardware as a terms violation serious enough to brick the camera. Whether the company would apply an equivalent standard to an internal product that achieves the same functional result through authorised design is a question regulators have not yet posed publicly. The three candidate enforcement bodies for privacy in camera-equipped AI eyewear — the EU AI Office under the AI Act’s Annex III high-risk classification, the Federal Trade Commission, and state attorneys general under Connecticut’s neural-data law — are each at a different posture: one deferred its enforcement clock by sixteen months, one is not moving on AI hardware, and one is enforcing a statute without yet naming a target.

Samsung Display Ends the G-VR Project, Closing the Near-Term Path to a Cheaper Vision Pro

Samsung Display has terminated its G-VR micro-OLED panel project, according to The Elec, with formal programme closure scheduled for September 2026. G-VR was Samsung Display’s development effort to produce a lower-cost micro-OLED panel that would supply a more affordable Apple Vision Pro successor. Micro-OLED’s cost has been the primary hardware barrier between the Vision Pro’s current price point and mass-market spatial computing: the displays account for a substantial fraction of the device’s bill of materials, and a cheaper Samsung Display alternative would have opened a path Apple’s own supply chain could not reach. MacRumors / The Elec 7/10

The cancellation forecloses a specific supply-chain option, not every option: Sony remains Apple’s current micro-OLED supplier, and alternative display technologies including tandem OLED could reach a Vision Pro successor on a different timeline. But the G-VR termination is the clearest market signal yet that a Vision Pro at a materially lower price point is not arriving through the Samsung Display route in the 2026–2027 window. Samsung Galaxy Glasses — also confirmed for Snapdragon AR1 and launching at Unpacked on 22 July — takes a different approach to the price problem: audio-only, no display, targeting $379–$499. The two Samsung products represent two different bets on where the market resolves, being made within the same company the same month.

Even Realities raises $150 million at a $1 billion valuation for camera-free smart glasses. The Beijing-based company closed a $150m round at a $1bn post-money valuation, backed by Tencent and Meituan, with CNBC reporting the deal on 6 July. Founder and CEO Will Wang was previously a hardware engineer on the Apple Watch and iPhone programmes. Even Realities’ glasses carry an AR display and audio capability but are architecturally camera-free by design — privacy through hardware omission rather than software policy or LED signalling. The round was announced in the same week that Meta’s Ray-Ban modifications attracted firmware enforcement. That timing is coincidental; the investment thesis is not. A segment of buyers, at unicorn-validated scale, will pay for device-layer privacy rather than relying on whatever the software layer provides. CNBC 9/10

Angelo’s Angle

Meta’s firmware detected and disabled a hardware modification designed to suppress a single LED. The “super sensing” prototype, as reported, builds a device for which no LED suppression would be needed — the disclosure mechanism is not part of the capture architecture. The enforcement apparatus looks for the unauthorised removal of a feature. The prototype removes the requirement for that feature from the design specification.

The three regulatory candidates you have been tracking — EU AI Office, state AG, FDA — each have an enforcement posture, but the “who can audit” question was always asked against devices that have a capture event with a detectable signature. What is the audit target when the capture is continuous and the activation event is architecturally absent? Before the next enforcement announcement, settle this in your own notes: which body, under which existing legal authority, could require Meta to disclose the capture behaviour of a shipping consumer device that has no LED to trigger?
