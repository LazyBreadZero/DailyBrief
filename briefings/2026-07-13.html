Neuralink Plans Fully Automated, Dura-Sparing BCI Surgery and High-Volume Production in 2026

Elon Musk announced this week that Neuralink will begin high-volume production of its implantable BCI device and transition to a fully automated surgical procedure in which electrode threads pass directly through the dura mater without first removing it. The dura is the tough outer membrane of the brain that has historically required a separate surgical incision and reclosure at each implant site, a step that added time, infection exposure, and a demand for neurosurgical judgment at the insertion stage. Passing threads through the intact dura without opening it eliminates that requirement, making the procedure's central steps reproducible by a surgical robot rather than reliant on a neurosurgeon's real-time adjustment.

The commercial significance is straightforward. By the most recent academic census, roughly 150 people worldwide carry any form of intracortical implant — a number that has moved slowly across the sector's entire history, Neuralink's PRIME trial included, which enrolled approximately 21 participants in two years of operation. The binding constraint has never been device performance or patient willingness. It has been surgical access: the requirement for a specialist neurosurgical team, a dedicated operating suite, and per-procedure management that no programme in the field can yet deliver at scale. A surgical protocol sufficiently standardised to run without bespoke neurosurgical adjustment at each step is what hospital deployment, rather than specialist-centre demonstration, actually requires. Automated, dura-sparing implantation does not remove the neurosurgeon from the room, but it removes the neurosurgeon from the rate-limiting position on the floor plan.

What it leaves unanswered is the question that comes after the patient leaves the operating suite. The BrainGate2 Nature Medicine paper published last month documented Casey Harrell running his speech BCI independently at home for more than 3,800 hours without researcher involvement — yet still requiring periodic software updates from the academic team. The academic team is not a commercial support model. Three constraints sit between current BCI capability and commercial deployment at anything resembling scale: surgical access, which Neuralink's dura-sparing robot begins to address; ongoing device maintenance and decoder support after implant, which no company has answered at commercial volume; and per-patient regulatory compliance per device, which the EU's Annex III clock and the US's emerging state neural-data rules make increasingly non-trivial. Neuralink now has a named architecture for the first. The second and third remain open to any of the three commercial players.

Angelo’s Angle

Three commercial BCI companies now have a named architecture for getting the device into a patient: Neuralink via automated robot surgery, Synchron via hospital partnership, Science Corp via ophthalmic-device retail. Each answers the access problem differently. None has publicly answered what happens in year two — who services the decoder, who maintains the firmware between approved updates, who the patient calls. Before your next deployment-readiness take, write one paragraph settling which of these three architectures makes the ongoing-support answer easiest to give. Then check whether the company with the most scalable implantation model is also the one with the most credible support model. They may not be the same company.
