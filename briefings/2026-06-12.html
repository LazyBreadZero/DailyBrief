Who Writes XR's History? AWE's Hall of Fame and the Politics of Institutional Memory

Friday, 12 June 2026 · ~1,050 words · Sources graded below

Three days before AWE USA 2026 opens its doors in Long Beach, the organisers released the conference's final pre-show packaging in a single announcement: eight new inductees to the XR Hall of Fame, the Art Festival's slate of nine award-winning works and four world premieres, a confirmed slot for Evan Spiegel's keynote immediately after opening remarks, and — new for 2026 — an expanded floor dedicated to location-based entertainment. Individually, each is a programming detail. Together, they are something else: in the space of one press release, a 2026 trade show acquired a history (the Hall of Fame), a canon (the Art Festival), and — building on Edition 8's new Research Poster Track — a scholarly apparatus. That is not what trade shows do. It is what institutions do.

A Hall of Fame Is an Argument About Origins

The XR Hall of Fame's 2026 class — among them Rebecca Allen, the VR artist whose 1980s computer-animated work predates almost everything AWE will exhibit this year; Karl Guttag, the AR display analyst whose technical teardowns have shaped how the trade press itself evaluates optics; Brett Leonard, director of The Lawnmower Man, the film that gave 1990s audiences their first mainstream image of VR; and Al Rodgers, credited with foundational motion-tracking work — is, on its face, a generous and defensible list. It follows an inaugural 2024 class of 101 pioneers and a further 10 in 2025. But a Hall of Fame is never just a list of names. It is an institution's answer to the question "where did we come from?" — and the answer it gives shapes, for everyone who encounters it afterward, what the field is.

Look at what the 2026 class spans, in AWE's own framing: "immersive art, motion tracking, AR hardware and display innovation, immersive storytelling, digital media, human factors and safety standards, and education." That is a coherent origin story for augmented and virtual reality as a visual and interaction medium — optics, tracking, content, safety standards for headsets. It is also, notably, a story with no neuroscience in it. No BCI pioneer, no clinical neurotech figure, nothing from the "consciousness tech" end of the convergence this briefing has spent eleven editions documenting — Apple's Wheelchair Control, Precision Neuroscience × Medtronic, Neuralink's expanding surgical scope, Science Corp's retinal implant. AWE's Research Poster Track (Edition 8) opened an academic on-ramp for neuroscience at the show. Its Hall of Fame, writing the field's history in the same week, has not yet found room for it.

Three Forms of Legitimacy, Arriving Together

Step back further. A field acquires legitimacy through (at least) three channels: commercial validation (products that ship and sell — Android XR's fall launch slate, Snap's Specs), cultural validation (art, film, criticism — the Art Festival's four world premieres), and historical/scholarly validation (an agreed account of who built what, when, and a venue for peer-reviewed research — the Hall of Fame and the Research Poster Track). Most fields acquire these slowly, through separate institutions, often over decades, and the resulting account of "the field's history" is contested precisely because no single actor controls all three channels.

AWE, this week, is moving toward controlling all three simultaneously — keynote stage, art festival, hall of fame, and academic track, under one roof, one brand, one theme ("I, Spatial — Humans Empowered by Spatial AI") that Edition 10 already noted has become the industry's own language for itself. This is not a conspiracy; it is what a successful trade show does as a field consolidates around it, and AWE's organisers would likely describe it as service to the community rather than as an act of narrative control. But the effect is the same regardless of intent: the account of "what XR is, where it came from, and what counts as its best work" that a newcomer encounters in 2026 is now substantially authored by one event, in one week, in Long Beach.

What This Means for a Convergence Thesis

Angelo's working thesis — the "post-screen human interface," holding XR, BCI, spatial computing, and consciousness tech as one convergence story — depends on these fields being read together. AWE's 2026 packaging is evidence that, at the level of institutional memory, they are not yet being read together: the show that increasingly defines "spatial computing" as a category has, in the same week, defined that category's history, art, and emerging scholarship in ways that include neuroscience as a guest (the Research Poster Track, two editions old) but not yet as a citizen (the Hall of Fame, eight inductees, none from neurotech).

This is not a criticism of AWE's choices — eight inductees cannot represent every adjacent field, and the Research Poster Track is itself only two editions old as an institution. It is a data point on a question this briefing has not yet asked directly: if Angelo's convergence thesis is correct, it currently runs ahead of the institutions that would ratify it. The translator's job, on this reading, is not just explaining the convergence to people who allocate capital and attention — it may be arguing for the convergence against an institutional account of the field's own history that does not yet contain it. Those are different jobs, and AWE's history-writing this week is the clearest evidence yet of which one Angelo is actually doing.

AWE Locks Its Final Shape — a Hall of Fame, an Art Festival, and a Keynote, All Three Days Before Doors Open

PR Newswire / AWE Official 10/10   PR Newswire / Snap 10/10   VR.org 7/10

AWE USA 2026 runs June 15–18 at the Long Beach Convention Center, and this week's announcement is the conference's final pre-show packaging in one release: eight new inductees to the XR Hall of Fame, the Art Festival's selections — nine multi-award-winning works plus four world premieres — and confirmation that Evan Spiegel's keynote, "Making Computing More Human," runs Tuesday June 16 from 9:30–10:00am PT, immediately after opening remarks from AWE co-founder Ori Inbar. A new expo area dedicated to location-based entertainment — VR attractions, arcades, immersive activations — joins the floor for the first time. AWE is now describing itself, in its own materials, as "an equal mix of technology conference, art festival, research summit, startup incubator, and cultural celebration."

The Hall of Fame's 2026 class includes Rebecca Allen, the VR artist whose 1980s computer-animated work predates the medium AWE exists to showcase; Karl Guttag, the AR display analyst whose optics teardowns shape how the trade press evaluates hardware; Brett Leonard, director of The Lawnmower Man, cinema's first mainstream VR image; and Al Rodgers, credited with foundational motion-tracking work. It follows an inaugural 2024 class of 101 pioneers and a further 10 inducted in 2025. AWE frames the 2026 class as spanning "immersive art, motion tracking, AR hardware and display innovation, immersive storytelling, digital media, human factors and safety standards, and education."

Step back: as introduced in Edition 8 (8 June 2026), AWE added a Research Poster Track this year — the first formal academic on-ramp for neuroscience and health-tech research at a major XR trade show. This week's announcement adds two more institutional layers in the same release: a Hall of Fame (an agreed account of the field's history) and an Art Festival (cultural legitimacy, juried and premiered). Most fields acquire commercial, cultural, and historical/scholarly legitimacy slowly, through separate institutions, often over decades. AWE is acquiring all three in roughly the same week, under one brand, with one theme — "I, Spatial," which Edition 10 already flagged as language the industry has adopted as its own. A trade show in 2024 sold booths. A trade show in 2026 is starting to write the field's history while selling the booths.

The new Location-Based Entertainment floor matters for a separate reason: as introduced in Edition 8, three consumer social-XR platforms (Spatial, Rec Room, Horizon Worlds) shut down or repositioned in the span of weeks. A dedicated arcade/attractions hall at AWE 2026 is a bet that "leave home to use this" XR — venues, not apps — is where consumer demand is migrating, even as the home-social layer collapses. It is the first physical-floor evidence at AWE that the displaced-creator-and-capital question Edition 8 left open has a candidate destination.

Notably absent from the Hall of Fame's 2026 class: anyone from neurotech or BCI. The Research Poster Track opened a door for neuroscience two editions ago; the institution writing the field's history this week has not yet walked through it. For a briefing built on the premise that XR and BCI are converging into one "post-screen human interface" story, that is worth holding onto going into Monday — AWE's own account of where the field came from does not yet include the half of the convergence this briefing spends half its time on.

"A trade show in 2024 sold booths. A trade show in 2026 is starting to write the field's history while selling the booths — and the history it's writing doesn't have neurotech in it yet."

Seoul's Smart-Glasses Showroom: the Comparison Culture Goes Global

South Korea's Ministry of Science and ICT ran the 2026 Korea Metaverse Festival at COEX in Seoul, June 10–12 — 140 companies across 270 booths, including a dedicated "smart glasses experience zone" where visitors tried Meta's Ray-Ban Meta, Samsung and Google's Galaxy XR, and Apple Vision Pro side by side. Vice Minister Ryu Je-myung's framing — that AI-and-XR convergence is becoming "a core growth engine across industries" — is government language, not vendor marketing, and a government building a head-to-head showroom for three competing ecosystems is itself a signal.

As introduced in Edition 6 (6 June 2026) and developed in Edition 11, the AR/XR price segmentation story — sub-$500 Android XR audio glasses vs. Snap's $2,500 prosumer display glasses — has so far been a US/EU pricing argument. A Seoul exhibition putting Ray-Ban Meta, Galaxy XR, and Vision Pro in the same room, for public comparison, before any of Edition 11's segmentation plays out commercially, suggests the "which ecosystem" question is being asked simultaneously in markets this briefing has not yet tracked.

Watch: whether Korean device-makers (Samsung already present via Galaxy XR) use this comparison-culture moment to announce their own AR-display hardware, separate from Samsung's existing Android XR partnership — a fourth ecosystem entrant would complicate Edition 11's two-market framing further.

BRAIN-COMPUTER INTERFACES

Neuralink Triples Its Electrode Count — the "Any Brain Region" Story Gets a Second Axis

Alongside the surgical-robot capability covered in Edition 11, Neuralink disclosed it is increasing its implant's electrode count from roughly 1,000 to 3,000 — aimed at improving neural signal capture — and is redesigning thread retention after early devices showed signal degradation over time. The company reports that, after design changes, 18 of the next 20 participants showed higher signal quality than earlier cohorts. Separately, Neuralink highlighted Audry, its first female participant, a spinal-cord-injury patient now producing visual art and planning a gallery show of work made via the implant.

As introduced in Edition 11 (11 June 2026), Neuralink's "any brain region" surgical capability redefines where the device can go — an expansion of addressable indications via robot and software, independent of new hardware approval. The electrode count increase is a different axis entirely: it is a hardware change to what the device can read once placed, requiring its own iteration cycle and presumably its own regulatory footprint. Two axes of expansion — surgical reach and signal resolution — moving on different timelines, on the same underlying platform, is a more complex regulatory object than "the same implant, more of them."

Watch: whether Neuralink pursues the 3,000-electrode design and the "any brain region" robotic capability as a single combined submission to FDA for a future indication, or sequences them — the choice will be the clearest evidence yet of how Neuralink itself thinks about its platform's regulatory unit (one device with evolving capability, vs. a family of related-but-distinct devices).

ANGELO'S ANGLE
Mentor's Provocation — two challenges, one contemplation. No task list.
PROVOCATION I · ANCHORED TO: NEURACLETECH'S "NEO" vs. EDITION 3'S NEUROXESS

Ten days ago, this briefing told you NeuroXess was the world's first commercially approved brain implant. This week, multiple outlets — including MIT Technology Review, not a tabloid — are making the identical claim about a different company, NeuracleTech's NEO, also NMPA-approved, also "first." Maybe these are the same approval reported under two names. Maybe they are two separate approvals, each marketed as "first" to different audiences. This briefing does not know, and — more to the point — Edition 3 did not flag that it didn't know.

Angelo's edge, per the brief he reads every morning, is supposed to be holding clinical, consumer, and platform threads at once while specialists see only their slice. But "the NMPA approved the world's first commercial BCI" is exactly the kind of claim that travels well precisely because it's simple — and exactly the kind that a Chinese regulatory disclosure environment, read only through English-language tech press, makes hardest to verify. If Angelo has been repeating "NeuroXess, world's first" in conversation since Edition 3, he has been repeating a claim this briefing itself cannot now confirm is even about a single, unambiguous event.

The question to settle in your own notes, not here: what is your actual verification standard for an NMPA claim, versus an FDA or CE-mark claim — and has it been the same standard, or have you been more credulous about China precisely because it's harder to check?

PROVOCATION II · ANCHORED TO: THE XR HALL OF FAME'S 2026 CLASS (TODAY'S LEAD)

AWE just inducted eight people into XR's official history — an artist, a display analyst, a film director, a motion-tracking pioneer. None from neurotech. None from BCI. The institution that increasingly defines "spatial computing" as a category (Edition 10's "I, Spatial" observation) has, in the same week, defined that category's history — and the convergence Angelo's entire positioning rests on isn't in it.

State, in 2-3 sentences, the strongest case that this is simply early — that BCI/neurotech pioneers will be inducted in future years as the Research Poster Track (two editions old) matures into a real presence, and AWE's institutional memory will catch up to the convergence the way it caught up to art and academia. Then state, in 2-3 sentences, the strongest case that this isn't a timing lag at all — that XR's history and BCI's history are, and will remain, two separate histories that only look like one convergence story from the specific vantage point Angelo has chosen to stand in.

If it's the second case — if "post-screen human interface" is Angelo's frame and not yet anyone else's, including the institutions that write the field's history — does that make his position more valuable, or does it mean he's translating a convergence that, institutionally, hasn't happened yet?

SIT WITH THIS · DO NOT RESOLVE BEFORE AWE OPENS MONDAY

Edition 11 left open whether "BCI" remains a useful word once brain access becomes general-purpose and software-defined. Today's lead raises a different version of the same kind of question, one level up: AWE this week became, in roughly one press release, the keeper of XR's official history, its cultural canon, and an emerging academic venue — all under a theme ("I, Spatial") that started as marketing copy and is now how the field describes itself, including, increasingly, how Angelo describes it.

Don't resolve this now. Let it sit through AWE: when a single commercial event accumulates the power to name a field's pioneers, certify its art, and host its emerging science — all while selling the products that field will buy — is that a sign of a field maturing normally, the way film festivals and professional societies once did for older industries? Or is it a sign that "spatial computing" doesn't yet have the independent institutions a mature field needs, and AWE is filling that vacuum because no one else has? You don't need an answer by Monday. You need to notice, walking the floor, which one it feels like.
