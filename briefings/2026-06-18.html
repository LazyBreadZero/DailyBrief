Snap’s $2,195 Bet Sends Its Stock to a 12-Month Low

Snap unveiled Specs, its first standalone AR glasses, on 16 June at AWE’s Long Beach stage, priced at $2,195. Investors answered the same day: the stock fell 8% to close at $4.74, its lowest level in a year, and stayed near that price into Wednesday — down roughly 30% over the past twelve months. The Motley Fool (8/10) TechCrunch (9/10)

Evan Spiegel defended the number on its own terms rather than against Meta’s cheaper Ray-Ban line or Apple’s pricier, bulkier Vision Pro: “The most important way to think of Specs is as a computer, and so they’re comparably priced to other high-end computers or high-end laptops.” An IDC research manager offered the rebuttal the market seemed to have already priced in, calling it “the worst time for any company to be launching any kind of premium product,” given Snap’s teenage-skewed user base and a balance sheet that still isn’t profitable on a GAAP basis. TechCrunch (9/10)

A day earlier, on the same stage, XREAL had opened reservations for AURA, its own Android XR glasses, while declining to name a price at all — committing only that the final figure “will not exceed” $1,500. For three years, AR hardware companies have set their own prices and waited years for adoption data to render a verdict. This week, for the first time, a real product met a public market willing to price its skepticism within twenty-four hours of the announcement — while the company building the cheaper alternative is still declining to commit to a number at all. PR Newswire / XREAL (9/10)

Angelo’s Angle

Spiegel calls Specs a computer, priced like one. The market, the most efficient sceptic you have access to, didn’t buy the comparison: Snap lost almost a third of its value this year before a single unit shipped. You have spent eighteen editions building the commercial case for this category. Steelman, in three sentences, the investor who sold on Wednesday — not the “they don’t get it yet” dismissal, but the strongest version of why $2,195 might simply be the wrong product at the wrong price, full stop.
