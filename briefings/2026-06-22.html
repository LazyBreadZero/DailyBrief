Valve’s Steam Frame Reaches US Warehouses as a Global Memory Shortage Holds Up Its Price

Valve’s first VR headset since the 2019 Index, the standalone Steam Frame, is sitting in the company’s US warehouses with no price attached. FCC paperwork made public on June 18th detailed the Frame’s two motion controllers, built around tunnelling-magnetoresistance thumbsticks (a different sensing technology from the Hall-effect sticks in Meta’s Quest controllers) and sold with an optional hot-swappable battery pack. Supply-chain tracker Brad Lynch separately logged roughly 35 tons of cargo marked “Virtual Reality Devices” arriving at Valve’s warehouses from Shanghai around June 10th, the same import-to-disclosure gap that preceded the Steam Controller’s May 4th launch. VR.org (7/10)

Valve confirmed via a Steam developer blog on June 4th that the Frame and its companion Steam Machine console remain scheduled for summer 2026, without naming a price for either. Designer Pierre-Loup Griffais has said only that the console will sit in line with current PC market rates and that the Frame is meant to undercut the $1,000 Index, guidance set last November, before memory costs moved. DRAM contract prices have risen more than 170 percent year on year since that announcement, as AI data centers absorb the bulk of global memory supply; Valve has already raised Steam Deck prices by as much as $300 this year to cover it. Engadget (9/10) Engadget (9/10)

The same cost line runs under every headset this brief has tracked this year. Snap’s $2,195 Specs were priced, and XREAL’s AURA was capped at a $1,500 ceiling with no final number yet, inside a memory market that Intel’s chief executive, Lip-Bu Tan, says will see no relief before 2028. A platform’s price tag, in 2026, increasingly reflects a memory invoice as much as a confidence signal. IEEE Spectrum (9/10)

Angelo’s Angle

You’ve spent the past week reading hardware price tags, Snap’s $2,195, XREAL’s $1,500 ceiling, as signals of how confident each company is in its own product. Valve’s stalled Steam Frame says a chunk of every 2026 spatial-computing price is being set upstream, by a memory shortage Intel’s CEO says won’t ease before 2028, not by demand. Which of this year’s price points can you still defend as demand-set rather than supply-set, and what evidence would tell you the difference?
