Spiegel Names the Category at AWE — and the Category Splits Into Three

Evan Spiegel took the AWE main stage in Long Beach this morning for a keynote titled “Making Computing More Human.” The phrase is now the commercial pitch for Snap Specs: binocular AR glasses, fall 2026 consumer launch, $2,500, Snap OS, OpenAI and Gemini integrated, 400,000 developers already building on the platform. The initial production run is constrained to roughly 100,000 units — a deliberate ceiling on a product entering a market nobody has proven at scale. Snap has spent $3 billion and eleven years getting here. The keynote is the public side of that bet.

The phrase matters as much as the hardware. AWE reporting published this week by Next Reality describes three distinct hardware tiers on the floor — AI camera eyewear ($329–499: Ray-Ban Meta, Android XR audio glasses), tethered AR glasses ($299–499: XREAL Aura, Acer GR0), and full standalone AR computers ($2,500: Specs) — each with a different capability ceiling and a different trust problem. A 2024 ACM study cited in the coverage found that the more convincingly a device resembles standard eyewear, the more effectively it conceals its recording capability from bystanders. The practical consequence: Tier 1, the most accessible tier and the one shipping in the highest unit volumes, carries the sharpest consent-risk per unit sold. Tier 3, the most capable, is also the most visible and the most expensive — which self-selects for an audience already prepared to engage with what they are wearing.

The three-tier split has structural implications beyond price. Each tier supports a different developer ecosystem, faces a different regulatory horizon, and rides a different adoption S-curve. Tier 1 is a fashion and convenience product that competes on price and form factor; Tier 2 is a professional and productivity device; Tier 3 — Specs, and eventually Apple’s display-forward waveguide glasses after 2029 — is a spatial computing platform competing on what it can place in space. These are not stages of the same market. They are three markets with a shared form factor and an overlapping vocabulary, which is precisely why “smart glasses” and “spatial computing” have been carrying more definitional weight than they could bear.

Spiegel’s keynote does not resolve the three-way split. What it does is name the category — “human-centric computing” — and anchor it to Tier 3 hardware at Tier 3 pricing. Android XR’s fall launch at $379–499 positions the same phrase at Tier 1. Apple’s N50, late 2027, will position it again. All three will call themselves human-centric computing. None of them is wrong. The question is what additional claim makes one of them more defensible than the others.

ALS Patient Uses BCI at Home for Two Years — Without a Researcher in the Room

A man living with ALS communicated more than 183,000 sentences and close to 2 million words over almost two years using an implanted brain-computer interface, without researcher support, at home. Average speed: 56 words per minute at 99% accuracy. The system, jointly developed by UC Davis, Brown University, and Mass General Brigham, is published this month in Nature Medicine. UC Davis (9/10) Medical Xpress (8/10)

The operative word is “independent.” Prior BCI demonstrations — including many of this briefing’s — have reported capability in controlled clinical settings, with a researcher calibrating the system and monitoring output. This trial ran unassisted at home for two years. That is a different claim: not “this works in a lab,” but “this person used it the way a wheelchair user uses a wheelchair.” Real-world longevity and independence data of this quality for any implanted BCI device has not previously existed in the published record.

The finding does not map cleanly onto the startup-regulatory race. The system is a research-developed implant from three academic medical centres. Its commercial significance is architectural: it provides empirical grounding for the claim that the “independent daily use” threshold — which is the threshold that matters for payer reimbursement and patient adoption — is achievable from implanted neural hardware today, without waiting for the next electrode-count milestone or the next surgical automation. The question of which commercial entity owns that finding, and when, is a separate calculation.

Angelo’s Angle

“Making Computing More Human” is now the headline of a $2,500 Snap product launch. This briefing has been watching that phrase build from positioning concept to category consensus. The question it leaves you: state, in one sentence, what you mean by human-centric computing that Spiegel could not print on a Specs announcement page. Not a richer version of the same claim — a different claim. If you cannot produce one cleanly, you are holding the phrase. Spiegel just shipped the hardware with it. What do you have that he doesn’t?
