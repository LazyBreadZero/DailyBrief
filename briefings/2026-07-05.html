A Speech Implant Ran at Home for 3,800 Hours Without a Researcher in the Room

Casey Harrell, a 47-year-old man with ALS, used a BrainGate2 intracortical brain-computer interface at home for more than 3,800 hours over nearly two years, communicating more than 183,000 sentences at an average of 56 words per minute, without researcher involvement. The study, published this week in Nature Medicine by researchers at UC Davis, Brown University, and Mass General Brigham, is the first peer-reviewed demonstration that an intracortical speech BCI can be operated independently by a patient on a near-daily basis, over a sustained real-world period. Nature Medicine 10/10

The design detail that makes this possible is decoder stability. Once trained, the core neural decoding model did not require daily recalibration. Harrell adjusted the system himself using a tablet interface; the research team provided only periodic remote software updates. At peak, the decoder translated intended speech at 56 words per minute. Over two years of near-daily independent sessions, he communicated close to two million words. The session count and duration together constitute something the field has not previously shown: a BCI functioning as a durable personal device, not as a laboratory protocol. UC Davis Health 9/10

The commercial significance is distinct from the clinical one. BrainGate2 is a university trial, not a product. But the result changes the burden of proof for every company building toward commercial deployment. The question was whether a patient could operate an intracortical BCI independently over months and years. It now has a documented answer: yes, on a near-daily basis, for nearly 3,800 hours, at 56 words per minute. The remaining question is not capability; it is infrastructure. Harrell still required periodic remote support from the research team. Translating that support model from a university trial to a commercial product — who answers the call when the decoder drifts, who maintains the software stack between FDA-cleared firmware releases — is a problem that Synchron, Neuralink, and Science Corp have not yet solved in public.

Further reporting

Meta releases Brain2Qwerty v2. On 1 July, Meta published a non-invasive neural-decoding system that converts brain activity into typed text without surgery. The v2 model achieves 61% word accuracy — versus roughly 8% for comparable non-invasive methods — using an end-to-end architecture trained on magnetoencephalography signals from nine participants, each spending about 10 hours in a $2 million MEG scanner. At 61% accuracy with non-portable hardware, it is not a near-term product. Meta’s bet is that non-invasive decoding can eventually reach the threshold where the implant route becomes a narrower clinical proposition. The v2 code is open-source. Road to VR 8/10 Dataconomy 7/10

Angelo’s Angle

The BrainGate2 result settles one question and opens a harder one. Casey Harrell operated a speech implant at home for nearly two years without a scientist present. That was the capability question, and it now has a documented answer. The infrastructure question follows immediately: Harrell’s BCI still required “periodic software updates” from the research team. When a commercial device leaves the hospital, who provides that support? Neuralink’s answer is an app platform; Synchron’s is a hospital partner network; Science Corp’s PRIMA is structured closer to an ophthalmic device with trained implanting surgeons. Each implies a different customer-success architecture, and none of them has been tested at scale.

Before you write your next piece on BCI deployment: write one paragraph — just one — on what first-line support looks like for a commercial speech BCI in year two of ownership. Who does the user call? What do they have authority to adjust? That paragraph will reveal whether any company has actually answered the question Harrell’s result now makes unavoidable.
