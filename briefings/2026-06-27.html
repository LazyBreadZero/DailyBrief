Apple Raises Vision Pro’s Price by $200, and Names the AI Memory Shortage as the Reason

Apple raised prices on fourteen products on June 25th — Mac, iPad, HomePod, HomePod mini, Apple TV and Vision Pro — citing a shortage of memory and storage chips it called unprecedented. Vision Pro’s three configurations rose $200 to $300: the 256GB model from $3,499 to $3,699, the 512GB from $3,699 to $3,899, and the 1TB from $3,999 to $4,199. The steepest jump on any product went to the Mac Studio M3 Ultra, from $3,999 to $5,299. iPhone, Apple Watch and AirPods prices were untouched. “The rapid expansion of AI data centers has created an extraordinary surge in demand for memory and storage,” Apple said. “We have never seen a component price increase this much, this quickly.” MacRumors (8/10) Al Jazeera (8/10)

Tim Cook had previewed the move eight days earlier, telling The Wall Street Journal that memory costs were a “hundred-year flood” he had “never seen… in any area in over 40 years,” and that Apple’s cash would go toward securing supply rather than building its own chip plants: “We can’t do everything. We know what we’re good at.” Microsoft raised Xbox prices by $100 to $150 the same week, saying console memory and storage costs had already risen more than 2.5 times and would roughly double again by autumn 2027 — confirmation that the constraint sits in the memory market generally, not in Apple’s supply chain specifically. MacRumors (8/10)

Apple shares fell 6.1 percent that day, the company’s worst single session since April 2025, even as some investors read the move as confirmation rather than alarm. Gene Munster called the sell-off an overreaction; Bloomberg Intelligence’s Mandeep Singh noted that rivals without Apple’s pricing power face the harder problem — demand destruction, if they try the same pass-through without the brand to absorb it. Yahoo Finance (7/10)

Three spatial-computing devices priced this month already pointed at the same mechanism without anyone at the companies saying so outright: Snap’s Specs, XREAL’s AURA and Valve’s Steam Machine all landed above earlier guidance, each citing the chip shortage by name, while Meta’s display-free glasses, the one device in the category without a costly compute-and-display stack, priced down the same week. Vision Pro fits the pattern at its extreme: the most display- and compute-dense headset on the market took one of the largest dollar increases of any Apple product, and this time the company doing the explaining was large enough, and transparent enough about its own input costs, to put a name and a number on what the cheaper devices had only implied.

On The Radar · Regulation

Connecticut joins the neural-data patchwork on July 1st. Its amended privacy law classifies neural data — information generated by measuring central or peripheral nervous-system activity — as sensitive personal data, requiring separate opt-in consent before it can be sold. It is the third state to do this, after Colorado and California both acted in 2024; Connecticut adds nothing structurally new, but it is the first deadline of its kind to land since the EU pushed its own high-risk AI deadline back to December 2027. Hunton Andrews Kurth (8/10)

Angelo’s Angle

Every cheaper headset this month let you infer the AI memory shortage from a price tag. Apple just confirmed it in a written statement, the week after Cook called it the worst component shock of his forty-year career. Munster says the market overreacted; Apple says the shortage itself is the part with no precedent. Pick one: is spatial computing’s hardware economics now hostage to a market it has zero influence over, or was a $3,699 headset already proof the category can absorb the cost, with Apple’s pricing power the exception rather than the warning?
