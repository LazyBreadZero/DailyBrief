Paradromics Implants Its First Human Brain-Computer Interface, Seven Months After FDA Clearance

Paradromics and University of Michigan Health completed the first human implantation of Connexus, the company’s wireless brain-computer interface, in a Michigan woman who has lost the ability to speak from motor neuron disease. The surgery, led by neurosurgeon Matthew Willsey, opens the Connect-One Early Feasibility Study, which the FDA cleared under an Investigational Device Exemption in November 2025 — two further sites, UC Davis and Massachusetts General, are enrolling alongside Michigan. The participant will be followed for six years. Paradromics (9/10) MassDevice (8/10)

Connexus packs 421 platinum-iridium microelectrodes, each under 40 microns wide, into a single cortical module, with the system designed to scale to four modules and 1,684 channels; a chest-implanted transceiver beams the decoded signal out through the skin over a 100 Mbit/s wireless link, with no external skull hardware. That channel count puts Connexus ahead of any BCI yet placed in a human, and the fully-implanted, wireless design is closer to what a paying user could plausibly tolerate for years than the cabled or skull-mounted rigs most academic trials still run. BioSpace (8/10)

The Connect-One trial’s lead investigator is David Brandman, the UC Davis neurosurgeon who, a week earlier, published the result that a BrainGate2 implant let an ALS patient communicate independently for two years with no researcher present. He now runs the academic gold-standard study and the commercial pivotal-track one for a different company’s very different hardware. Paradromics joins Neuralink, Synchron and China’s NMPA-approved NEO as the fourth distinct architecture now living inside a human skull, each pursuing a separate regulatory track toward the same destination: speech and computer control restored without a keyboard.

Angelo’s Angle

Brandman spent two years proving a BrainGate implant works with no researcher in the room, then took the lead-investigator seat on a venture-backed company’s commercial trial. Same expertise, different funder. When Angelo calls a BCI claim “independently validated,” is he checking that the validator had no stake in the outcome, or just that a credentialed name signed off? Write down the actual test, not the one he assumes he runs.
