Meta Launches $299 Smart Glasses, Undercutting Its Own Ray-Ban Meta Line by $80

Meta introduced three new smart glasses on Tuesday — Adventurer, Fury, and Starfire — sold for the first time under Meta’s own name rather than Ray-Ban’s or Oakley’s. Adventurer and Fury start at $299; Starfire, designed with Kylie Jenner, at $399. The cheaper two undercut the second-generation Ray-Ban Meta Wayfarer, which Meta itself prices at $379, by $80. TechCrunch (9/10) MacRumors (8/10)

All three share the same hardware: a 12-megapixel camera shooting 3K video, a five-microphone array, eight hours of battery life extended by 40 more from the charging case, and 26 colour-and-lens combinations compatible with prescriptions from −12 to +2.25 dioptres. They ship running Muse Spark, the first AI model out of Meta Superintelligence Labs built specifically for Meta’s own hardware, which adds 14 languages to live translation, bringing the total to 20, including Mandarin, Hindi, Korean and Japanese. Meta Newsroom (9/10)

Asked about Apple’s own glasses, expected in 2027, Meta’s head of wearables Alex Himel called the company “formidable” — “I think you need to take anything they do seriously” — then described what Apple is actually expected to ship: a camera, microphones and Siri, with no integrated display. That is a description of the tier Meta’s new glasses already occupy, not the display-led tier Meta’s own roadmap has been chasing. Himel’s quote concedes, in public, where Apple’s 2027 product competes with Meta today — and where it does not yet.

The pricing cuts against the year’s dominant hardware story. Snap’s Specs launched at $2,195, XREAL’s AURA still won’t commit below a $1,500 ceiling, and Valve priced the Steam Machine at $1,050 to $1,430 last week, all three citing the same global DRAM shortage. Road to VR (7/10) Meta’s glasses carry no display, no waveguide optics, and no console-grade memory; their bill of materials is a camera, a microphone array and a battery, and their price went down while everyone else’s went up. The shortage is squeezing compute- and display-heavy hardware specifically, not eyewear electronics in general — the first clear counter-example to a pricing story that, until now, had only run in one direction.

Angelo’s Angle

Himel’s quote did more than reassure investors: it told you which tier of Apple’s 2027 product Meta thinks it already beats, and which it doesn’t yet touch. You’ve read “three tiers” as a settled taxonomy for months. Himel just used it to concede ground, in public, on the record. Where does your own read of Meta’s roadmap put it relative to the tier it just ceded — and what would it actually cost Meta to close that gap?
