Today’s Lead · Policy & Regulation

EU Exempts Smart Glasses from Replaceable-Battery Rules — Just as Meta Designs Out the Camera Indicator

On 14 July, the European Commission adopted a delegated act under the EU Battery Regulation exempting six wearable categories — smart glasses, smartwatches, fitness trackers, smart earbuds, smart headphones, and medical wearables — from the requirement that consumers be able to replace their device’s battery themselves. Batteries must still be removable by a professional technician using standard tools; the exemption removes the end-user disassembly requirement only. European Commission 10/10

The six categories map almost exactly onto Meta’s current and forthcoming wearable product line. Meta’s Ray-Ban Meta smart glasses are sealed; their battery has never been user-accessible. The original requirement, which came into force last year for most consumer electronics, would have forced a redesign of every sealed wearable heading for the EU market. Politico reported that Washington flagged the original requirement to Brussels as a technical trade barrier — a detail that locates the exemption within the broader US-EU trade negotiation of early 2026, not solely in consumer safety engineering. Politico 7/10

The timing coincides with reporting on Meta’s next-generation smart glasses programme. Devices codenamed “Aperol” and “Bellini” — described as a “super sensing” prototype capable of periodic photography and continuous audio capture — are reported to eliminate the white LED indicator that blinks on current Ray-Ban Meta glasses whenever the camera is active. The current LED is the only passive signal visible to bystanders that a device is capturing. The next-generation design removes it. Fortune 7/10 9to5Google 7/10

The Battery Regulation’s delegated act settled one question: who may open a sealed device. It said nothing about what a sealed device may do while closed. Meta’s firmware update v26 for current glasses added a software-level response to users who had physically disabled the LED indicator — a workaround that itself confirmed the indicator was removable. The next-generation hardware makes its absence the factory default. Two decisions, same week, pointing in the same direction: sealed wearables become legally normalised in the EU, and their capture state becomes less visible by design.

Also Noted · AI Hardware

OpenAI’s First Device Is a Screenless AI Companion — Not a Wearable

Bloomberg’s Mark Gurman reported on 14 July that OpenAI’s first standalone consumer device — built with Jony Ive’s io design studio — is a pocket-sized AI companion with a microphone, camera, and speaker, and no screen. The price target is $200–$300, with a February 2027 ship window. Bloomberg / Gurman 8/10

The device is not a wearable and is not a smart-glasses competitor. It sits on a table or in a pocket; it has no head-worn form factor. For the three-layer stacking thesis tracked since Edition 30: OpenAI holds BCI-input middleware (Merge Labs), cross-platform AI-agent software in public beta, and now a companion device. None of the three constitutes a wearable-layer position in 2026. The February 2027 ship date puts it roughly concurrent with Apple’s N50 glasses target and six months after Meta’s Aperol/Bellini timeline. OpenAI’s live competitive pressure on the wearable market remains entirely at the AI-agent middleware layer, not at the device layer.

Angelo’s Angle

The EU Battery Regulation’s delegated act settles who may open a sealed wearable: a technician, not the user. Meta’s next-generation hardware settles whether bystanders can see when it is capturing: they cannot. Both decisions moved in the same week, and they are not in conflict — the Battery Regulation addresses the device’s interior; the LED removal addresses its exterior signal. The EU has a body that writes battery law (the Commission), a body that classifies AI systems as high-risk (the AI Office), and national data-protection authorities that enforce GDPR on data already captured. Name the institution that currently holds enforcement mandate over what a sealed wearable captures in real time — before the data leaves the device — and state what authority it has to act.
